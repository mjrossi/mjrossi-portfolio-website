I've never been someone who likes to sit too long on bureaucratic tasks. When I moved to Chicago in 2013, I went the next available business day to the Illinois Secretary of State to exchange my driver's license. I did the same in 2019, walking over to the nearest Department of Motor Vehicles office in Brooklyn.

So upon moving to Portugal, I jumped on all the paperwork right away. What I found was a system that felt much more like my identity lived in a normalized database.

## The hodgepodge of the US identity system

As an engineer, I recently worked on an identity and authorization system. From day one, I knew I would need two key pieces: a canonical record and a single source of truth.

The US has neither. So it is genuinely startling to move into a system that has something closer to both — albeit far from perfect. In the US, a driver's license here, a lease there, a utility bill standing in as proof that "this person exists at this address": all of it is accepted as commonplace.

### The driver's license

The driver's license is an interesting case study to open with: for many Americans it is their sole piece of identification, and the one most likely to tie together their identity and their residency. Since the ability to drive is effectively required to function in most of the US, most people have one. There is no general concept of a federally issued ID outside of a few cases — a passport, a passport card, a military ID — and since many Americans don't travel outside the country, they don't bother to acquire a passport. For a US-born citizen, the driver's license (or less often, a state ID) becomes a pivotal document.

This falls apart very quickly, though. Many people move but keep their old state driver's license until it expires. Rarely is an address on a driver's license in a state like New York — where people change apartments often — their actual current address. So the question of "who you are" is usually answered by the license, while "where you live" may not be anymore.

### The Social Security number

More than anything else, the Social Security number is the closest thing you have to a unique identifier. The problem? It was never designed for how it's currently used. There is clear value in having a federal-level identifier for an individual person, and the Social Security number is one of the only options that exists.

There isn't any sort of easily accessible way for someone to validate that a Social Security number is being used by the individual whose number it is. There isn't a database that a bank can connect to in order to verify it. State databases have it, but don't make it accessible. This means institutions have to validate it somehow themselves, poking at credit reports, background checks, identity verification systems, and the like. In practice, this makes the Social Security number function as a username and password, and it's assumed that if you know it, you are who you say you are.

### What about where you live?

There isn't a single, authoritative source that answers "where you live." As mentioned above, a driver's license falls apart on that question — even though, at the moment it is issued, it is usually the most authoritative answer available, since proving your address is often a requirement to obtain one.

Each institution you deal with — banks, the Internal Revenue Service, the Social Security Administration, and many others — asks you for an address, but there is no single place to point at that validates it. There is no document in the US that definitively answers "this is where I live."

## Comparison with the EU

Even before relocating to Portugal, I learned that the EU answers these identity questions very differently. Just by virtue of being an Italian citizen, I found that identity documents and citizen identifiers were far more formalized than in the US. Once I acquired Italian citizenship, I was automatically registered in a system called the Anagrafe degli Italiani Residenti all'Estero (AIRE) — a registry of every Italian citizen living abroad. As a citizen registered in AIRE, I deal with the consular district covering my residence for passports, registering life events, voting, and the rest.

Having just moved abroad myself, I can say there is no US equivalent to AIRE. There is no way for me to formally tell the various systems that I no longer live in the country. Worse, plenty of US institutions will simply stop doing business with you once you aren't residing there, which, if your stay abroad is temporary, becomes a real problem. Most expats work around it by maintaining a "legal domicile" address — a family member's, for example — and a mailing address, in order to keep their US ties intact. It is another patchwork built on the fragmented federal and state delineation that makes the US function, often enough, as "50 mini countries under a trenchcoat."

There are also explicit identification documents, where identity is the entire point — not health, not driving, not anything else. For Italy, that is the Carta d'Identità Elettronica (CIE). The card has an NFC chip inside that I can use with an app on my phone to authenticate to government services easily, and because the physical card has to be present, it is secure. The number on the card is 100% uniquely tied to me as a citizen, and anyone who needs to validate it can access the Italian government system, which also can (at times) cross EU borders, since the CIE meets the Regulation (EU) 2019/1157 as all EU Member State National identity cards are required.

Broadly, documents such as a driver's license or a health card are issued for their specific purpose and nothing more. They don't serve the same dual function as the US driver's license.

## Relocating to Portugal as an Italian citizen

For me, as an Italian citizen legally residing in an EU member state (Portugal) for studies, I couldn't just sign a lease and move in. You are required to register your presence with the municipality you live in. That differs sharply from the US, where I could move to New York and — unless I wanted a New York driver's license — go a long time without telling anyone I lived there, right up until I had to file a New York tax return.

### The Certificado de Registo de Cidadão da União Europeia (CRUE)

The Certificado de Registo de Cidadão da União Europeia (CRUE) is Portugal's implementation of the legal foundation allowing any EU citizen to reside in Portugal (specifically Directive 2004/38/EC, which establishes the right of EU citizens and their family members to move and reside freely within the EU).

Obtaining it requires a specific list of documents. I arrived at the office with my Italian passport, proof of financial means, proof of enrollment at university, and proof of my residential address. I received the certificate immediately, and I watched the data go into a government system accessible to anyone who needs to validate it. I can go to a website run by the Portuguese government myself, enter a public access code printed on the document (free to share), and confirm that it's legitimate.

### The Número de Identificação Fiscal (NIF)

The Número de Identificação Fiscal (NIF) is a fiscal, or taxpayer, identification number. It is analogous to the US Social Security number, except that this number isn't meant to be secret and you can hand it to anyone. I went to the pet store the afternoon we arrived, and they asked me for it. I was so confused — why would a store want this? I assumed that, like my Social Security number, it should be kept secret. Apparently not, and there are reasons.

The number is linked to Finanças, the Portuguese tax authority. Its job is to identify you financially. The number alone won't get anyone anywhere, so you can hand it out freely. It is a username only, whereas the Social Security number often has to act as both username and password. In Portugal, the password is your Cartão de Cidadão (for Portuguese citizens), or the CRUE (in my case).

The US has no equivalent second credential that's universally trusted, so institutions started treating "knows the SSN" as good enough proof of identity. Over decades it got quietly promoted from identifier to de facto password. That's the actual flaw: one field doing two jobs it was never built for.

## The normalized identity database

Ultimately, the takeaway that I found going through the registration was that the combination of my Italian identity document, the CRUE, and the NIF, all come together to form a normalized, easily accessible database of who I am to the government and the various systems that need to interact with it. It becomes a fairly straightforward "key -> value" pair that can answer the question "who am I, where do I live".

Contrast that with the US, where a collection of loosely linked proxies substitutes for that structure — where the Social Security number, the driver's license, and often some one-off document have to come together every time you answer the same question.

The Portuguese system is far from perfect. The document list can be left up to interpretation, and it doesn't cross EU borders, so this is a process that has to be repeated. Each EU member state implements the directive differently, so while the core tenets are the same, the actual implementation differs. It's still not as connected as it could be across agencies and services — updating my address, for instance, would still have to be done in several places.

Still, as an engineer I found this system and structure to be fairly easy to wrap my head around and appreciate. Now that REAL ID in the US is actually being enforced, I will be curious if more of these systems start to do a better job of communicating with each other — hopefully for the benefit of citizens and residents.
