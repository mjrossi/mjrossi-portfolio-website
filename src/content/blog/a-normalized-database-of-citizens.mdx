I recently relocated to Lisbon, and in doing so I jumped immediately on the bureaucratic task of properly establishing my residency and acquiring the respective documentation. I had acquired Italian citizenship before the move, so I arrived in Portugal as an EU citizen rather than an American one. I've never been someone who likes to sit too long on bureaucratic tasks. What I found was a system that felt much more like my identity lived in a normalized database.

Portugal, Italy, and the wider European Union have taken more concrete steps to build out a normalized database of citizens. The US has too, but only for immigrants — without the benefits that I've found accessible so far in the EU.

## What a system needs

As an engineer, I recently worked on an identity and authorization system. From day one, I knew I would need two key pieces: a canonical record and a single source of truth.

Quick level setting on definitions: a canonical record is one entry that is the person, with an identifier stable enough that every other system can point at it. A single source of truth is narrower and per-fact: one place that owns each specific fact about that person. Every other copy is fed from that source, so a disagreement is an error condition rather than the normal state of affairs.

The US has neither for its citizens. Instead, it has authoritative records organized around purpose. It requires cross-referencing several items to get all the specifics for a person. In the US, a driver's license here, a lease there, a utility bill standing in as proof that "this person exists at this address": all of it is accepted as commonplace. It was genuinely startling to move into a system that has something closer to both — albeit far from perfect. For non-citizens, though, the US does have both.

Strip the problem down and there are only three questions any of this has to answer: who you are, what proves it, and where you live. Every document below is one country's answer to one of them.

## Who you are

The Portuguese Número de Identificação Fiscal (NIF) is a fiscal, or taxpayer, identification number. It is analogous to the US Social Security number, except that this number isn't meant to be secret and you can hand it to anyone. I went to the pet store the afternoon we arrived, and they asked me for it. I was so confused — why would a store want this? I assumed that, like my Social Security number, it should be kept secret.

Apparently not, and the reason turns out to be the thesis of this entire post. The number is linked to Finanças, the Portuguese tax authority, and giving it at the register attaches that receipt to my tax record. Where it lands depends on what I bought: health, education, and housing feed a deduction against the tax itself, while restaurants, garages, and gyms return a percentage of the IVA I paid. Either way the state is holding the receipt, and it has the classification pre-filled before I ever open the return — no shoebox to reconcile in the spring. The pet store was asking because *I* benefit.

Italy does the same, and I learned it years ago. Just by virtue of being an Italian citizen, I found that identity documents and citizen identifiers were far more formalized than in the US. My codice fiscale — the Italian equivalent of a Social Security number, issued by the Agenzia delle Entrate — identifies me permanently, and it is printed on my identity card without being issued by it.

My instinct to guard the number came from growing up in the US. More than anything else, the Social Security number is the closest thing an American has to a unique identifier. What makes the NIF and the codice fiscale safe to hand out is that the number alone won't get anyone anywhere. Each is a username and nothing more, whereas the US Social Security number has to act as both username and password. The US has no equivalent second credential that's universally held, so institutions started treating "knows the SSN" as good enough proof of identity. Over decades it got quietly promoted from identifier to de facto password. That's the actual flaw: one field doing two jobs it was never built for.

The first Social Security numbers were issued in 1936. For most of this number's life, there was no way at all for a bank to check that the person reciting it was the person it belonged to. That finally changed in 2020, when the Social Security Administration launched [eCBSV](https://www.ssa.gov/dataexchange/eCBSV/), a verification service that Congress had to legislate into existence in 2018 specifically to fight synthetic identity fraud. The API arrived eighty-four years later, and even now it is narrow: consent-gated, fee-based, limited to financial institutions and their service providers, and it answers only whether a name, a date of birth, and a number match SSA's records. That confirms the number belongs to that name. It does not confirm that the person in front of you *is* that name.

So there is still no general-purpose lookup, just a handful of purpose-built ones — eCBSV for banks, SSNVS for employers reporting wages, SSOLV for states issuing driver's licenses. Two of those lanes go further than the rest. E-Verify, the check that confirms a new hire is allowed to work, puts a government-held photo in front of the employer — the exact thing eCBSV cannot do — though it is the employer who compares it to the document, by eye. And it only works when the document handed over is a green card, an Employment Authorization Document, a US passport, or a passport card. SAVE does something similar for state agencies that need to confirm someone's immigration status. Both hang off a federally issued document rather than the Social Security number. That is why a citizen carrying nothing but a state driver's license never encounters either one. Everyone outside those lanes validates by inference instead: credit reports, background checks, commercial identity verification.

There is clear value in having a federal-level identifier for an individual person, and for a citizen the Social Security number is one of the only options that exists. Non-citizens get a purpose-built one. The Department of Homeland Security assigns non-citizens an A-number, short for alien registration number. Unlike the Social Security number, it was built to be an identifier and nothing else — it follows a person through every change of status, and onto the naturalization certificate if they become a citizen. For years it reached only part of that population: a green card holder had one, a tourist or a student typically didn't. That gap closed this summer. A [DHS rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/29/2026-13057/alien-registration-form-and-evidence-of-registration) finalized in June 2026 requires effectively every non-citizen aged fourteen or over who stays thirty days to register, be fingerprinted, and be issued a number if they don't already have one.

## What proves it

For most American citizens, the answer to identity verification is a driver's license. This document is the one most likely to tie together their identity and their residency. Since the ability to drive is effectively required to function in most of the US, most people have one. Federal ID is the exception rather than the rule — a passport, a passport card, a military ID — and since many Americans don't travel outside the country, they don't bother to acquire a passport. The driver's license (or less often, a state ID) becomes the pivotal document.

Europeans tend to carry identification documents, where identity is the entire point — not health, not driving, not anything else. For Italy, that is the Carta d'Identità Elettronica (CIE). It is governed by two EU instruments: [Directive 2004/38/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2004/38/oj/eng) requires every member state to issue its nationals an identity card or a passport, and requires the other member states to accept it, but says nothing about what that document looks like. Uniformity came later, with [Regulation (EU) 2019/1157](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R1157), which defines common security standards and harmonization for national identity cards.

The card is not yet how most Italians actually log in. More than forty million authenticate to government services with SPID, a credential issued independently of any identity document. In autumn 2025 the government decided to retire it, and the transition now under way points at the CIE and the IT-Wallet instead. The CIE carries my unique identifier — the codice fiscale — and an NFC chip that authenticates me directly. Since the physical card has to be present, it satisfies the security requirement: the codice fiscale is my username, the CIE is my password. Once SPID is gone, that pairing is the only way in. Italy is collapsing its credentials back onto a single document — normalization playing out in real time.

Portugal implements its own version of this. Citizens receive a Cartão de Cidadão. The rest of us use a passport or residence title tied to us. Regardless of identity card, the NIF acts as our username, the document verifies our identity, and the password is a state-certified credential — most commonly the [Chave Móvel Digital](https://www.autenticacao.gov.pt/a-chave-movel-digital), though the Cartão de Cidadão's own certificates and an eIDAS credential from another member state authenticate just as well. The fundamental principle is the same.

The US license has to do both jobs at once, and it falls apart very quickly on the second. Many people move but keep their old state driver's license until it expires. Often enough, the address on a driver's license in a state like New York — where people change apartments frequently — is no longer where that person lives. So the question of "who you are" is usually answered by the license, while "where you live" may not be anymore.

US non-citizens, meanwhile, have a very different situation. United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) issues several federal photo IDs. The permanent resident card (Form I-551, known colloquially as a green card) is the obvious example, and the Employment Authorization Document (Form I-766) is another. 8 U.S.C. § 1304(e) requires any registered non-citizen 18 or older to carry their registration document at all times, and failing to do so is a misdemeanor. No US citizen has any comparable duty. So the country that supposedly has no ID card does issue one — and only mandates carrying it for the people who are not citizens.

## Where you live

For me, as an Italian citizen legally residing in an EU member state (Portugal) for studies, signing a lease was only the first half of arriving. Free movement gives any EU citizen an unconditional right to be in Portugal for three months; past that, you are required to register your presence with the municipality you live in, within thirty days of the three-month mark.

The document that records it is the Certificado de Registo de Cidadão da União Europeia (CRUE), Portugal's implementation of [Directive 2004/38/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2004/38/oj/eng) — the same instrument that requires Italy to issue me an identity card also establishes my right to move to Portugal and live here. One directive, answering two of the three questions.

Obtaining it requires a specific list of documents. I arrived at the office with my Italian passport, proof of financial means, proof of enrollment at university, and proof of my residential address. I received the certificate immediately, and I watched the data go into a government system accessible to anyone who needs to validate it. I can go to a [website](https://check.aima.gov.pt/doc/Verificacao.aspx) run by the Portuguese immigration authority Agência para a Integração, Migrações e Asilo (AIMA), enter the access code printed on the document (free to share), and confirm that it's legitimate.

My wife on the other hand is not an EU citizen, so her pathway has a different set of frictions. Being married to an EU citizen still gets her an expedited process, but she has to go through AIMA directly — slower, with more conditions attached, and more of them left to interpretation. The route is nothing like mine. The destination is: the same three questions, answered in the same normalized structure, on the same join key.

That differs sharply from the US, where I could move to New York and, unless I wanted a New York driver's license, go a long time without telling anyone I lived there, right up until I had to file a New York tax return. The US produces many answers to the question "where do you live," and each individual one is authoritative to that institution. If you last filed a federal tax return living in Illinois, the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) has that address until you file again, or submit a specific IRS change of address request. The Social Security Administration may have a different address for you. All of these are authoritative, but there is no single source of truth.

Unless, again, you're an immigrant! Form AR-11 requires a change of address within 10 days of moving (8 U.S.C. § 1305), and failing to file is a criminal offense; for students and exchange visitors, the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System (SEVIS) does the same job, with the school reporting on their behalf. So the mandatory federal residence register exists, but only for foreigners.

## When you leave

Italy keeps track even after you leave. Once I acquired Italian citizenship from abroad, I was enrolled as part of that process in a system called the Anagrafe degli Italiani Residenti all'Estero (AIRE) — a registry of every Italian citizen living abroad. It is a declaration rather than an automatic consequence of citizenship; an Italian who moves abroad later has to file it themselves. As a citizen registered in AIRE, I deal with the consular district covering my residence for passports, registering life events, voting, and the rest.

Having just moved abroad myself, I can say there is no US equivalent to AIRE for citizens. Instead, the US keeps track of citizens living abroad indirectly, and far more invasively, through the tax code. The US is one of only two countries that tax on the basis of citizenship at all — Eritrea is the other, with a flat 2% levy on its diaspora — and the only one that applies its full domestic income tax code to citizens wherever they live.

Where that differs from a formalized system like AIRE is that the IRS knows I live abroad, but until I specifically tell anyone else, they don't. Worse, plenty of US institutions will simply stop doing business with you once you aren't residing there, which, if your stay abroad is temporary, becomes a real problem. Most expats work around it by maintaining a "legal domicile" address — a family member's, for example — and a mailing address, in order to keep their US ties intact. It is another patchwork built on the fragmented federal and state delineation that makes the US function, often enough, as "50 mini countries under a trenchcoat."

Unless, once more, you're not a citizen. For a non-citizen, leaving is itself a filing: a permanent resident who gives up the status submits Form I-407, and USCIS is required by law to report it to the IRS. Until then the registration obligations keep running, and they are backed by criminal statute rather than by a tax bill. Taxation for citizens, plus criminal penalties for foreigners.

## The normalized identity database

Ultimately, the takeaway from the whole process of registering in Portugal was seeing the shape of it. My Italian identity document, the CRUE, and the NIF are not three separate copies of me across multiple databases. Logically, they're three tables — one answering who I am, one proving it, and one recording where I live. That's what normalization means in a database: you don't duplicate a fact, you point at the one place it lives, so there is never a second copy to go quietly out of date.

As an engineer, I found the European approach easy to wrap my head around and easy to appreciate. Portugal and Italy have made my administrative life straightforward. It also came with tangible benefits: the pet-store receipt, and every other shopping trip, fed into my tax return; one ANPR write propagating to five agencies automatically; my CRUE issued the same day, with online verification anyone can access; and eIDAS, an authentication credential from one member state that can be used in another. How close a country gets to that shape is the real difference between the three systems in this piece.

Italy comes closest of the three. The register is the ANPR (Anagrafe Nazionale della Popolazione Residente), owned by the Ministry of the Interior. A change of residence is *one* write — online through the ANPR portal — and it propagates automatically to Agenzia delle Entrate, INPS, INAIL, ISTAT and the Motorizzazione. Those agencies still keep their own records, so this is replication rather than a true foreign key. What matters is that every copy is fed from one authoritative write, so a divergence is a fault rather than the expected state. It is a first step toward what the EU calls the "once-only principle": you tell the state something once, and the state carries it from there.

Portugal comes close in that the data is the right shape. The NIF acts as a stable join key, and there is an authoritative owner for each administrative domain (Finanças for tax, the câmara for residence, AIMA for immigration, and so on), but those systems don't share a common canonical record. It's essentially a normalized schema where the foreign keys aren't entirely connected. Updating my address, for instance, would still have to be done in several places. It falls short in other ways too: the document list can be left up to interpretation, and the registration itself doesn't cross EU borders, so moving to another member state means starting over. Each member state implements the directive differently, so while the core tenets are the same, the actual implementation differs. The upside is that the key and the authentication mechanism are consistent. [eIDAS](https://eidas.ec.europa.eu/efda/home) is the piece of this I have actually leaned on: a credential issued by one member state authenticates to another's services, with nothing re-issued. Authentication travels; registration doesn't.

The US system is denormalized for citizens. My address is *copied* into the DMV, the IRS, the Social Security Administration, my bank, and my utility company — each an independent write, with nothing propagating between them. Copies drift. The stale address on that New York driver's license isn't an administrative failure; it's an update anomaly, the exact thing normalization exists to prevent. So a loose collection of proxies stands in for the structure, and the Social Security number, the driver's license, and often some one-off document have to be assembled together every time you answer the same question. This adds administrative friction to citizens and how they interact with the government.

Meanwhile, non-citizens get the opposite. One identifier — the A-number — that USCIS, Customs and Border Protection, the State Department and the immigration courts all file against. Mandatory registration on arrival, mandatory address writes within ten days, mandatory status reporting, and a formal exit record in Form I-407 when permanent residency is abandoned — a filing USCIS is required by law to report to the IRS. Those agencies keep their own systems, so this is nearly the mirror image of Italy: a real join key, and propagation that fires at the boundaries — registration, status change, exit — rather than running continuously under every fact the way ANPR does. A non-citizen gets the join key and the propagation, but is billed for it in duties and obligations that no citizen carries, nor any of the potential benefits.

[REAL ID](https://www.tsa.gov/real-id) has been enforced in the US since May 2025, and it is a genuine step — to issue a compliant license, a state now has to check the applicant's Social Security number against the SSA's records rather than take the card at face value. But look at what each applicant gets checked against: a citizen's status rests on a birth certificate or a passport, a non-citizen's on a live federal database called SAVE. Even here, the same split. What REAL ID standardizes is how the states issue their own documents; it doesn't build the thing underneath them, at least not for citizens. Even within this, looking at the Transportation Security Administration (TSA) as an example, travelers who don't have a compliant ID have been able since February 2026 to pay $45 for a process called "TSA ConfirmID." The fee buys a ten-day window rather than a single trip, and TSA is careful to say it cannot promise the check will succeed. Without a general-purpose federal citizen identity record to check against, the federal response was a paid, inference-based verification lane that may still not produce an answer.

The same pattern shows up at the airport. TSA PreCheck and Global Entry are the expedited lanes for domestic screening and for coming back into the country, respectively, and getting into either one means an application, a fee, a background check, fingerprints, and an in-person interview. A citizen who wants a faster identity check has to volunteer for a small version of the registration regime non-citizens are compelled into, and pay for it, in five-year increments. My EU passport does the same job for free. There is no application because there is nothing to apply for: the chip authenticates against a record that already exists — the codice fiscale and the CIE again, username and password. In one system, trust in a citizen is manufactured one traveler at a time and sold. In the other, it is a property of the record.

## Who it was built for

The US does have a normalized identity database — canonical record, stable join key, one authoritative owner, mandatory propagation on change — it just lives in the immigration system. For its own citizens, the country has refused to build this type of population register.

From 1946 to 1972, the SSN card carried "NOT FOR IDENTIFICATION." The Privacy Act of 1974 stopped government agencies from demanding the number, and said nothing about anyone else — which is why a landlord, a bank, and a doctor's office can all still insist. Something as straightforward as REAL ID took two decades to overcome state resistance.

The US built exactly one population register and pointed it at people who cannot vote. Normalization is not politically neutral.

I walked through the EU citizen door in Portugal and had a certificate the same afternoon. I walked through the citizen door in America and got a filing cabinet. Same person, two paths, and the path is what determined the schema I experienced. None of that is something I earned.

An immigrant in Portugal would have a different process to build their database, but once it is built, it would look much like mine. An immigrant in the US would have neither my process nor my end result: far more is recorded about them at the federal level than any US citizen is required to hold.

I'll be curious whether these systems ever start talking to each other in earnest. The EU is trying — there's a digital identity wallet coming that would carry all of this across borders, and I'll sign up the day it ships.

The US could stand to take a page out of the European book, but needs to do so in a way that properly protects the civil liberties of the population. That objection is why it was never built — and it has already failed on its own terms: the register got built anyway, and pointed at the people with the least protection from it. Refusing to build one for citizens never prevented the surveillance; it only withheld the half that helps. Without those protections, it'll end up a surveillance state for citizens and immigrants both, and nothing more.
