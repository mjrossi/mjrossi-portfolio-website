I've never been someone who likes to sit too long on bureaucratic tasks. When I moved to Chicago in 2013, I went the next available business day to the Illinois Secretary of State to exchange my driver's license. I did the same in 2019, walking over to the nearest Department of Motor Vehicles office in Brooklyn.

So upon moving to Portugal, I jumped on all the paperwork right away. What I found was a system that felt much more like my identity lived in a normalized database.

## The hodgepodge of the US identity system

As an engineer, I recently worked on an identity and authorization system. From day one, I knew I would need two key pieces: a canonical record and a single source of truth.

The US has neither. So it is genuinely startling to move into a system that has something closer to both — albeit far from perfect. In the US, a driver's license here, a lease there, a utility bill standing in as proof that "this person exists at this address": all of it is accepted as commonplace.

### The driver's license

The driver's license is an interesting case study to open with: for many Americans it is their sole piece of identification, and the one most likely to tie together their identity and their residency. Since the ability to drive is effectively required to function in most of the US, most people have one. There is no general concept of a federally issued ID outside of a few cases — a passport, a passport card, a military ID — and since many Americans don't travel outside the country, they don't bother to acquire a passport. For a US-born citizen, the driver's license (or less often, a state ID) becomes a pivotal document.

This falls apart very quickly, though. Many people move but keep their old state driver's license until it expires. Often enough, the address on a driver's license in a state like New York — where people change apartments frequently — is no longer where that person lives. So the question of "who you are" is usually answered by the license, while "where you live" may not be anymore.

### The Social Security number

More than anything else, the Social Security number is the closest thing you have to a unique identifier. The problem? It was never designed for how it's currently used. There is clear value in having a federal-level identifier for an individual person, and the Social Security number is one of the only options that exists.

For most of this number's life, there was no way at all for a bank to check that the person reciting it was the person it belonged to. That finally changed in 2020, when the Social Security Administration launched [eCBSV](https://www.ssa.gov/dataexchange/eCBSV/), a verification service that Congress had to legislate into existence in 2018 specifically to fight synthetic identity fraud.

It's worth sitting with that timeline. The first Social Security numbers were issued in 1936. The API arrived eighty-four years later, and even now it is narrow: consent-gated, fee-based, limited to financial institutions and their service providers, and it answers only whether a name, a date of birth, and a number match SSA's records. That confirms the number belongs to that name. It does not confirm that the person in front of you *is* that name.

So there is still no general-purpose lookup, just a handful of purpose-built ones — eCBSV for banks, SSNVS for employers reporting wages, SSOLV for states issuing driver's licenses. Everyone outside those lanes validates by inference instead: credit reports, background checks, commercial identity verification. In practice, this makes the Social Security number function as a username and a password at once, and it's assumed that if you know it, you are who you say you are.

### What about where you live?

There isn't a single, authoritative source that answers "where you live." As mentioned above, a driver's license falls apart on that question — even though, at the moment it is issued, it is usually the most authoritative answer available, since proving your address is often a requirement to obtain one.

Each institution you deal with — banks, the Internal Revenue Service, the Social Security Administration, and many others — asks you for an address, but there is no single place to point at that validates it. There is no document in the US that definitively answers "this is where I live."

## Comparison with the EU

Even before relocating to Portugal, I learned that the EU answers these identity questions very differently. Just by virtue of being an Italian citizen, I found that identity documents and citizen identifiers were far more formalized than in the US. Once I acquired Italian citizenship, I was automatically registered in a system called the Anagrafe degli Italiani Residenti all'Estero (AIRE) — a registry of every Italian citizen living abroad. As a citizen registered in AIRE, I deal with the consular district covering my residence for passports, registering life events, voting, and the rest.

Having just moved abroad myself, I can say there is no US equivalent to AIRE. There is no way for me to formally tell the various systems that I no longer live in the country. Worse, plenty of US institutions will simply stop doing business with you once you aren't residing there, which, if your stay abroad is temporary, becomes a real problem. Most expats work around it by maintaining a "legal domicile" address — a family member's, for example — and a mailing address, in order to keep their US ties intact. It is another patchwork built on the fragmented federal and state delineation that makes the US function, often enough, as "50 mini countries under a trenchcoat."

There are also explicit identification documents, where identity is the entire point — not health, not driving, not anything else. For Italy, that is the Carta d'Identità Elettronica (CIE). The card has an NFC chip inside that I can use with an app on my phone to authenticate to government services easily, and because the physical card has to be present, it is secure.

The split underneath it is the part I keep coming back to. The number printed on the card identifies the *card* — renew it and that number changes. What identifies *me*, permanently, is the codice fiscale, which the card carries but doesn't originate. One field for who I am, a separate one for the thing that proves it. And because the CIE conforms to [Regulation (EU) 2019/1157](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R1157) — a common security standard for the national identity cards member states issue, though Denmark and Ireland sit outside it — that proof travels across most EU borders.

Broadly, documents such as a driver's license or a health card are issued for their specific purpose and nothing more. They don't serve the same dual function as the US driver's license.

## Relocating to Portugal as an Italian citizen

{/* FACT CORRECTION. The draft said "I couldn't just sign a lease and move in."
You can - Directive 2004/38/EC gives any EU citizen an unconditional right of

For me, as an Italian citizen legally residing in an EU member state (Portugal) for studies, signing a lease was only the first half of arriving. Free movement gives any EU citizen an unconditional right to be in Portugal for three months; past that, you are required to register your presence with the municipality you live in, within thirty days of the three-month mark. That differs sharply from the US, where I could move to New York and — unless I wanted a New York driver's license — go a long time without telling anyone I lived there, right up until I had to file a New York tax return.

### The Certificado de Registo de Cidadão da União Europeia (CRUE)

The Certificado de Registo de Cidadão da União Europeia (CRUE) is Portugal's implementation of the legal foundation allowing any EU citizen to reside in Portugal (specifically [Directive 2004/38/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32004L0038), which establishes the right of EU citizens and their family members to move and reside freely within the EU).

Obtaining it requires a specific list of documents. I arrived at the office with my Italian passport, proof of financial means, proof of enrollment at university, and proof of my residential address. I received the certificate immediately, and I watched the data go into a government system accessible to anyone who needs to validate it. I can go to a website run by the Portuguese government myself, enter a public access code printed on the document (free to share), and confirm that it's legitimate.

The Número de Identificação Fiscal (NIF) is a fiscal, or taxpayer, identification number. It is analogous to the US Social Security number, except that this number isn't meant to be secret and you can hand it to anyone. I went to the pet store the afternoon we arrived, and they asked me for it. I was so confused — why would a store want this? I assumed that, like my Social Security number, it should be kept secret.

Apparently not, and the reason turns out to be the argument in miniature. The number is linked to Finanças, the Portuguese tax authority, and giving it at the register attaches that receipt to my tax record through a system called e-fatura. Spending on things like restaurants, health, education, and general retail then feeds my income tax deductions automatically, with no shoebox of receipts to reconcile in the spring. The pet store was asking because *I* benefit.

That only works because the number alone won't get anyone anywhere. It is a username and nothing more, whereas the Social Security number has to act as both username and password. In Portugal the password is a separate thing entirely: the Cartão de Cidadão for Portuguese citizens, and for the rest of us the [Chave Móvel Digital](https://www.autenticacao.gov.pt/a-chave-movel-digital), a state-certified credential tied to a phone number and to a passport or residence title.

The US has no equivalent second credential that's universally trusted, so institutions started treating "knows the SSN" as good enough proof of identity. Over decades it got quietly promoted from identifier to de facto password. That's the actual flaw: one field doing two jobs it was never built for.

Ultimately, the takeaway from the whole registration was that my Italian identity document, the CRUE, and the NIF are not three copies of me. They are three tables. Each fact about my existence has exactly one authoritative home — who I am, where I live, what I owe — and everything else holds a reference and looks it up. That's what normalization means in a database: you don't duplicate a fact, you point at the one place it lives, so there is never a second copy to go quietly out of date.

The US system is the denormalized one. My address is *copied* into the DMV, the IRS, the Social Security Administration, my bank, and my utility company — each an independent write, with nothing propagating between them. Copies drift. The stale address on that New York driver's license isn't an administrative failure; it's an update anomaly, the exact thing normalization exists to prevent. So a loose collection of proxies stands in for the structure, and the Social Security number, the driver's license, and often some one-off document have to be assembled together every time you answer the same question.

The Portuguese system is far from perfect. The document list can be left up to interpretation, and it doesn't cross EU borders, so this is a process that has to be repeated. Each EU member state implements the directive differently, so while the core tenets are the same, the actual implementation differs. It's still not as connected as it could be across agencies and services — updating my address, for instance, would still have to be done in several places.

Still, as an engineer, I found this system easy to wrap my head around and easy to appreciate. [REAL ID](https://www.tsa.gov/real-id) has been enforced in the US since May 2025, and it is a genuine step — to issue a compliant license, a state now has to verify the applicant's Social Security number against the SSA's records rather than take the card at face value. But it standardizes how the states issue their own documents. It doesn't build the thing underneath them. I'll be curious whether these systems start communicating with each other in earnest — hopefully for the benefit of citizens and residents, and not only for the institutions checking up on them.
