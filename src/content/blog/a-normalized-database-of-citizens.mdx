I've never been someone who likes to sit too long on bureaucratic tasks. When I moved to Chicago in 2013, I went the next available business day to the Illinois Secretary of State to exchange my driver's license. I did the same in 2019, walking over to the nearest Department of Motor Vehicles office in Brooklyn.

I had acquired Italian citizenship before the move, so I arrived in Portugal as an EU citizen rather than an American one and, true to form, jumped on all the paperwork right away. What I found was a system that felt much more like my identity lived in a normalized database.

## What a system needs

As an engineer, I recently worked on an identity and authorization system. From day one, I knew I would need two key pieces: a canonical record and a single source of truth.

The US has neither for a person. Instead, it has authoritative records organized around purpose. It requires cross-referencing several items to get all the specifics for a person. It was genuinely startling to move into a system that has something closer to both at the definition of the individual person — albeit far from perfect. In the US, a driver's license here, a lease there, a utility bill standing in as proof that "this person exists at this address": all of it is accepted as commonplace.

Strip the problem down, though, and there are only three questions any of this has to answer: who you are, what proves it, and where you live. Every document below is one country's answer to one of them.

## Who you are

The Portuguese Número de Identificação Fiscal (NIF) is a fiscal, or taxpayer, identification number. It is analogous to the US Social Security number, except that this number isn't meant to be secret and you can hand it to anyone. I went to the pet store the afternoon we arrived, and they asked me for it. I was so confused — why would a store want this? I assumed that, like my Social Security number, it should be kept secret.

Apparently not, and the reason turns out to be the thesis to this entire post. The number is linked to Finanças, the Portuguese tax authority, and giving it at the register attaches that receipt to my tax record through a system called e-fatura. Spending on things like restaurants, health, education, and general retail then feeds my income tax deductions automatically, with no shoebox of receipts to reconcile in the spring. The pet store was asking because *I* benefit.

That instinct to guard the number came from growing up in the US. More than anything else, the Social Security number is the closest thing an American has to a unique identifier. The problem? It was never designed for how it's currently used. There is clear value in having a federal-level identifier for an individual person, and the Social Security number is one of the only options that exists.

For most of this number's life, there was no way at all for a bank to check that the person reciting it was the person it belonged to. That finally changed in 2020, when the Social Security Administration launched [eCBSV](https://www.ssa.gov/dataexchange/eCBSV/), a verification service that Congress had to legislate into existence in 2018 specifically to fight synthetic identity fraud.

The first Social Security numbers were issued in 1936. The API arrived eighty-four years later, and even now it is narrow: consent-gated, fee-based, limited to financial institutions and their service providers, and it answers only whether a name, a date of birth, and a number match SSA's records. That confirms the number belongs to that name. It does not confirm that the person in front of you *is* that name.

So there is still no general-purpose lookup, just a handful of purpose-built ones — eCBSV for banks, SSNVS for employers reporting wages, SSOLV for states issuing driver's licenses. Everyone outside those lanes validates by inference instead: credit reports, background checks, commercial identity verification.

Years ago, I learned that the EU answers these questions very differently. Just by virtue of being an Italian citizen, I found that identity documents and citizen identifiers were far more formalized than in the US. My codice fiscale — the Italian equivalent of a Social Security number, issued by the Agenzia delle Entrate — identifies me permanently, and it is printed on my identity card without being issued by it.

What makes the NIF and the codice fiscale safe to hand out is that the number alone won't get anyone anywhere. Each is a username and nothing more, whereas the Social Security number has to act as both username and password. The US has no equivalent second credential that's universally trusted, so institutions started treating "knows the SSN" as good enough proof of identity. Over decades it got quietly promoted from identifier to de facto password. That's the actual flaw: one field doing two jobs it was never built for.

## What proves it

For many Americans, the best answer to identity verification is a driver's license. This document is the one most likely to tie together their identity and their residency. Since the ability to drive is effectively required to function in most of the US, most people have one. There is no general concept of a federally issued ID outside of a few cases — a passport, a passport card, a military ID — and since many Americans don't travel outside the country, they don't bother to acquire a passport. For a US-born citizen, the driver's license (or less often, a state ID) becomes a pivotal document.

There are also explicit identification documents, where identity is the entire point — not health, not driving, not anything else. For Italy, that is the Carta d'Identità Elettronica (CIE). It is governed by two EU instruments: [Directive 2004/38/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2004/38/oj/eng) requires every member state to issue its nationals an identity card or a passport, and requires the other member states to accept it, but says nothing about what that document looks like. Uniformity came later, with [Regulation (EU) 2019/1157](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R1157), which defines common security standards and harmonization for national identity cards.

For Italy, the card carries my unique identifier — the codice fiscale — and an NFC chip that lets me authenticate to government services. Since the physical card has to be present, it satisfies the security requirement: the codice fiscale is my username, the CIE is my password, and both are required for accessing government services.

Portugal implements its own version of this. For citizens, they receive a Cartão de Cidadão. The rest of us use a passport or residence title tied to us. Regardless of identity card, the NIF acts as our username, the document verifies our identity, and then the password everyone authenticates against is the [Chave Móvel Digital](https://www.autenticacao.gov.pt/a-chave-movel-digital), a state-certified credential. The fundamental principle, and the underlying EU regulations, are the same.

The US license has to do both jobs at once, and it falls apart very quickly on the second. Many people move but keep their old state driver's license until it expires. Often enough, the address on a driver's license in a state like New York — where people change apartments frequently — is no longer where that person lives. So the question of "who you are" is usually answered by the license, while "where you live" may not be anymore.

## Where you live

For me, as an Italian citizen legally residing in an EU member state (Portugal) for studies, signing a lease was only the first half of arriving. Free movement gives any EU citizen an unconditional right to be in Portugal for three months; past that, you are required to register your presence with the municipality you live in, within thirty days of the three-month mark.

The document that records it is the Certificado de Registo de Cidadão da União Europeia (CRUE), Portugal's implementation of the legal foundation allowing any EU citizen to reside there (specifically [Directive 2004/38/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32004L0038), which establishes the right of EU citizens and their family members to move and reside freely within the EU).

Obtaining it requires a specific list of documents. I arrived at the office with my Italian passport, proof of financial means, proof of enrollment at university, and proof of my residential address. I received the certificate immediately, and I watched the data go into a government system accessible to anyone who needs to validate it. I can go to a [website](https://check.aima.gov.pt/doc/Verificacao.aspx) run by the Portuguese immigration authority Agência para a Integração, Migrações e Asilo (AIMA), enter the access code printed on the document (free to share), and confirm that it's legitimate.

That differs sharply from the US, where I could move to New York and, unless I wanted a New York driver's license, go a long time without telling anyone I lived there, right up until I had to file a New York tax return. The US produces many answers to the question "where do you live", and each individual one is authoritative to that institution. If you last filed a federal tax return living in Illinois, the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) has that address until you file again, or submit a specific IRS change of address request. The Social Security Administration may have a different address for you. All of these are authoritative, but there is no single source of truth.

Italy keeps track even after you leave. Once I acquired Italian citizenship, I was automatically registered in a system called the Anagrafe degli Italiani Residenti all'Estero (AIRE) — a registry of every Italian citizen living abroad. As a citizen registered in AIRE, I deal with the consular district covering my residence for passports, registering life events, voting, and the rest.

Having just moved abroad myself, I can say there is no US equivalent to AIRE. There is no way for me to formally tell the various systems that I no longer live in the country. Worse, plenty of US institutions will simply stop doing business with you once you aren't residing there, which, if your stay abroad is temporary, becomes a real problem. Most expats work around it by maintaining a "legal domicile" address — a family member's, for example — and a mailing address, in order to keep their US ties intact. It is another patchwork built on the fragmented federal and state delineation that makes the US function, often enough, as "50 mini countries under a trenchcoat."

## The normalized identity database

Ultimately, the takeaway from the whole process of registering in Portugal was seeing the shape of it. My Italian identity document, the CRUE, and the NIF are not three separate copies of me across multiple databases. They are three tables — one answering who I am, one proving it, and one recording where I live. That's what normalization means in a database: you don't duplicate a fact, you point at the one place it lives, so there is never a second copy to go quietly out of date. How close a country gets to that is the real difference between the three systems in this piece.

Italy comes closest of the three. The register is the ANPR (Anagrafe Nazionale della Popolazione Residente), owned by the Ministry of the Interior. A change of residence is *one* write — online through the ANPR portal — and it propagates automatically to Agenzia delle Entrate, INPS, INAIL, ISTAT and the Motorizzazione. Those agencies still keep their own records, so this is replication rather than a true foreign key. What matters is that every copy is fed from one authoritative write, so none of them can drift. It is a first step toward what the EU calls the "once-only principle": you tell the state something once, and the state carries it from there.

Portugal comes close in that the data is the right shape. The NIF acts as a stable join key, and there is one authoritative owner per fact (Finanças for tax, the câmara for residence, AIMA for immigration, and so on), but those systems don't share a common canonical record. It's essentially a normalized schema where the foreign keys aren't entirely connected. Updating my address, for instance, would still have to be done in several places. It falls short in other ways too: the document list can be left up to interpretation, and none of it crosses EU borders, so the process has to be repeated. Each member state implements the directive differently, so while the core tenets are the same, the actual implementation differs. The upside is that at least the key and the authentication mechanism are consistent.

The US system is entirely denormalized. My address is *copied* into the DMV, the IRS, the Social Security Administration, my bank, and my utility company — each an independent write, with nothing propagating between them. Copies drift. The stale address on that New York driver's license isn't an administrative failure; it's an update anomaly, the exact thing normalization exists to prevent. So a loose collection of proxies stands in for the structure, and the Social Security number, the driver's license, and often some one-off document have to be assembled together every time you answer the same question.

Still, as an engineer, I found the European approach easy to wrap my head around and easy to appreciate. [REAL ID](https://www.tsa.gov/real-id) has been enforced in the US since May 2025, and it is a genuine step — to issue a compliant license, a state now has to verify the applicant's Social Security number against the SSA's records rather than take the card at face value. But it standardizes how the states issue their own documents. It doesn't build the thing underneath them. Even within this, looking at the Transportation Security Administration (TSA) as an example, travelers who don't have a compliant ID can pay $45 to go through a separate "TSA ConfirmID" process. With no canonical record to check, the federal response was to build a paid, inference-based verification lane.

I'll be curious whether these systems start communicating with each other in earnest — hopefully for the benefit of citizens and residents, and not only for the institutions checking up on them.
