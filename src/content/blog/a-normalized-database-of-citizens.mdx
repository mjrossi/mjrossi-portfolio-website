I've never been someone who likes to sit too long on bureaucratic tasks. When I moved to Chicago in 2013, I went the next available business day to the Illinois Secretary of State to exchange my driver's license. I did the same in 2019, walking over to the nearest Department of Motor Vehicles office in Brooklyn.

I had acquired Italian citizenship before the move, so I arrived in Portugal as an EU citizen rather than an American one and, true to form, jumped on all the paperwork right away. What I found was a system that felt much more like my identity lived in a normalized database.

## What a system needs

As an engineer, I recently worked on an identity and authorization system. From day one, I knew I would need two key pieces: a canonical record and a single source of truth.

## Who you are

The Número de Identificação Fiscal (NIF) is a fiscal, or taxpayer, identification number. It is analogous to the US Social Security number, except that this number isn't meant to be secret and you can hand it to anyone. I went to the pet store the afternoon we arrived, and they asked me for it. I was so confused — why would a store want this? I assumed that, like my Social Security number, it should be kept secret.

Apparently not, and the reason turns out to be the thesis to this entire post. The number is linked to Finanças, the Portuguese tax authority, and giving it at the register attaches that receipt to my tax record through a system called e-fatura. Spending on things like restaurants, health, education, and general retail then feeds my income tax deductions automatically, with no shoebox of receipts to reconcile in the spring. The pet store was asking because *I* benefit.

That instinct to guard the number came from growing up in the US. More than anything else, the Social Security number is the closest thing an American has to a unique identifier. The problem? It was never designed for how it's currently used. There is clear value in having a federal-level identifier for an individual person, and the Social Security number is one of the only options that exists.

For most of this number's life, there was no way at all for a bank to check that the person reciting it was the person it belonged to. That finally changed in 2020, when the Social Security Administration launched [eCBSV](https://www.ssa.gov/dataexchange/eCBSV/), a verification service that Congress had to legislate into existence in 2018 specifically to fight synthetic identity fraud.

The first Social Security numbers were issued in 1936. The API arrived eighty-four years later, and even now it is narrow: consent-gated, fee-based, limited to financial institutions and their service providers, and it answers only whether a name, a date of birth, and a number match SSA's records. That confirms the number belongs to that name. It does not confirm that the person in front of you *is* that name.

So there is still no general-purpose lookup, just a handful of purpose-built ones — eCBSV for banks, SSNVS for employers reporting wages, SSOLV for states issuing driver's licenses. Everyone outside those lanes validates by inference instead: credit reports, background checks, commercial identity verification.

Even before relocating to Portugal, I learned that the EU answers these questions very differently. Just by virtue of being an Italian citizen, I found that identity documents and citizen identifiers were far more formalized than in the US. My codice fiscale — the Italian equivalent of a Social Security number, issued by the Agenzia delle Entrate — identifies me permanently, and it is printed on my identity card without being issued by it.

What makes the NIF and the codice fiscale safe to hand out is that the number alone won't get anyone anywhere. Each is a username and nothing more, whereas the Social Security number has to act as both username and password. The US has no equivalent second credential that's universally trusted, so institutions started treating "knows the SSN" as good enough proof of identity. Over decades it got quietly promoted from identifier to de facto password. That's the actual flaw: one field doing two jobs it was never built for.

## What proves it

For many Americans, the driver's license is their sole piece of identification, and the one most likely to tie together their identity and their residency. Since the ability to drive is effectively required to function in most of the US, most people have one. There is no general concept of a federally issued ID outside of a few cases — a passport, a passport card, a military ID — and since many Americans don't travel outside the country, they don't bother to acquire a passport. For a US-born citizen, the driver's license (or less often, a state ID) becomes a pivotal document.

There are also explicit identification documents, where identity is the entire point — not health, not driving, not anything else. For Italy, that is the Carta d'Identità Elettronica (CIE). The card has an NFC chip inside that I can use with an app on my phone to authenticate to government services easily, and because the physical card has to be present, it is secure. The number printed on it identifies the card rather than me — renew it and that number changes. And because the CIE conforms to [Regulation (EU) 2019/1157](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R1157) — a common security standard for the national identity cards member states issue — that proof travels across most EU borders.

So the codice fiscale is essentially my username, and the CIE is my password. Both are required. Portugal implements its own version of the same split — the Cartão de Cidadão for Portuguese citizens, and for the rest of us the [Chave Móvel Digital](https://www.autenticacao.gov.pt/a-chave-movel-digital), a state-certified credential tied to a phone number and to a passport or residence title — but the fundamental principle, and the underlying EU regulation, is the same.

The US license has to do both jobs at once, and it falls apart very quickly on the second. Many people move but keep their old state driver's license until it expires. Often enough, the address on a driver's license in a state like New York — where people change apartments frequently — is no longer where that person lives. So the question of "who you are" is usually answered by the license, while "where you live" may not be anymore.

## Where you live

For me, as an Italian citizen legally residing in an EU member state (Portugal) for studies, signing a lease was only the first half of arriving. Free movement gives any EU citizen an unconditional right to be in Portugal for three months; past that, you are required to register your presence with the municipality you live in, within thirty days of the three-month mark.

The document that records it is the Certificado de Registo de Cidadão da União Europeia (CRUE), Portugal's implementation of the legal foundation allowing any EU citizen to reside there (specifically [Directive 2004/38/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32004L0038), which establishes the right of EU citizens and their family members to move and reside freely within the EU).

Obtaining it requires a specific list of documents. I arrived at the office with my Italian passport, proof of financial means, proof of enrollment at university, and proof of my residential address. I received the certificate immediately, and I watched the data go into a government system accessible to anyone who needs to validate it. I can go to a website run by the Portuguese government myself, enter a public access code printed on the document (free to share), and confirm that it's legitimate.

That differs sharply from the US, where I could move to New York and, unless I wanted a New York driver's license, go a long time without telling anyone I lived there, right up until I had to file a New York tax return. There isn't a single, authoritative source that answers the question. As above, a driver's license falls apart on it — even though, at the moment it is issued, it is usually the most authoritative answer available, since proving your address is often a requirement to obtain one.

Each institution you deal with — banks, the Internal Revenue Service, the Social Security Administration, and many others — asks you for an address, but there is no single place to point at that validates it. There is no document in the US that definitively answers "this is where I live."

Italy keeps track even after you leave. Once I acquired Italian citizenship, I was automatically registered in a system called the Anagrafe degli Italiani Residenti all'Estero (AIRE) — a registry of every Italian citizen living abroad. As a citizen registered in AIRE, I deal with the consular district covering my residence for passports, registering life events, voting, and the rest.

Having just moved abroad myself, I can say there is no US equivalent to AIRE. There is no way for me to formally tell the various systems that I no longer live in the country. Worse, plenty of US institutions will simply stop doing business with you once you aren't residing there, which, if your stay abroad is temporary, becomes a real problem. Most expats work around it by maintaining a "legal domicile" address — a family member's, for example — and a mailing address, in order to keep their US ties intact. It is another patchwork built on the fragmented federal and state delineation that makes the US function, often enough, as "50 mini countries under a trenchcoat."

Ultimately, the takeaway from the whole registration was that my Italian identity document, the CRUE, and the NIF are not three copies of me. They are three tables. Each fact about my existence has exactly one authoritative home — who I am, where I live, what I owe — and everything else holds a reference and looks it up. That's what normalization means in a database: you don't duplicate a fact, you point at the one place it lives, so there is never a second copy to go quietly out of date.

The US system is the denormalized one. My address is *copied* into the DMV, the IRS, the Social Security Administration, my bank, and my utility company — each an independent write, with nothing propagating between them. Copies drift. The stale address on that New York driver's license isn't an administrative failure; it's an update anomaly, the exact thing normalization exists to prevent. So a loose collection of proxies stands in for the structure, and the Social Security number, the driver's license, and often some one-off document have to be assembled together every time you answer the same question.

It's still not as connected as it could be across agencies and services — updating my address, for instance, would still have to be done in several places.

Still, as an engineer, I found this system easy to wrap my head around and easy to appreciate. [REAL ID](https://www.tsa.gov/real-id) has been enforced in the US since May 2025, and it is a genuine step — to issue a compliant license, a state now has to verify the applicant's Social Security number against the SSA's records rather than take the card at face value. But it standardizes how the states issue their own documents. It doesn't build the thing underneath them. I'll be curious whether these systems start communicating with each other in earnest — hopefully for the benefit of citizens and residents, and not only for the institutions checking up on them.
