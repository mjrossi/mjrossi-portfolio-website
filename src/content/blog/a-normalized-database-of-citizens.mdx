I recently relocated to Lisbon, and in doing so I jumped immediately on the bureaucratic task of properly establishing my residency and acquiring the documents that go with it. I had obtained Italian citizenship before the move, so I arrived in Portugal as an EU citizen rather than an American one. I've never been someone who likes to sit too long on bureaucratic tasks. What I found was a system that felt much more like my identity was stored in a normalized database. Portugal, Italy, and the wider European Union have taken more concrete steps toward something closer to a normalized identity infrastructure. The US has too, but only for immigrants — without the benefits that I've found accessible so far in the EU. 

A key component in writing software is designing the data model. Data models don't just live in software though: they're all around us. We interact with systems, and systems generate data, that is stored, referenced, and updated. As I went through the tasks I needed to do upon arriving here, and I started obtaining documents, seeing how my data was registered, and comparing it to the US, I saw a data model of a resident in a city. I wanted to explore the contract between the model I was familiar with and the one I had just relocated into. That deep dive is this blog post.

## What a system needs

As an engineer, I recently worked on an identity and authorization system. From day one, I knew I would need two key pieces: a canonical record and a single source of truth.

Two definitions first: a canonical record is one entry. In this case, that entry is the resident. The resident has an identifier, which needs to be stable enough that every other system can point at it. A single source of truth means that one place that owns a specific fact about that resident, and every other copy is fed from that source. If there is a disagreement between two systems, being able to rely on a single source of truth allows it to be resolved quickly instead of having to try and decide which is correct.

By that standard the US has neither for its citizens. It keeps authoritative records organized around each agency's own purpose instead, so pulling together the specifics on a person means cross-referencing several of them: a driver's license here, a lease there, a utility bill standing in as proof that "this person exists at this address." All of it accepted as commonplace. It was startling to move into a system that has something closer to both — a canonical record and a single source of truth — albeit far from perfect. For non-citizens, though, the US does have both.

Strip the problem down and there are only three questions any identity system has to answer: who you are, what proves it, and where you live.

## Who you are: the identifier

The Portuguese Número de Identificação Fiscal (NIF) is a fiscal, or taxpayer, identification number. It is analogous to the US Social Security number. I went to the pet store the afternoon we arrived, and they asked me for it. I was so confused — why would a store want this? I assumed that, like my Social Security number, it should be kept secret.

Apparently not, and the reason turns out to be the thesis of this entire post. The number is linked to Finanças, the Portuguese tax authority, and giving it at the register files that receipt into e-fatura, the invoice record Finanças keeps against my NIF. How it counts depends on what I bought: health, education, and housing come off the tax itself, while restaurants, garages, and gyms return a percentage of the IVA I paid. Either way the state is holding the receipt, and it has the classification pre-filled as much as possible before I ever open my tax return — no physical shoebox of receipts to reconcile in the spring. The pet store was asking because *I* benefit.

Italy does the same, and I learned it years ago. Italian identity documents and citizen identifiers are far more formalized than the US equivalents. My codice fiscale — the Italian equivalent of a Social Security number, issued by the Agenzia delle Entrate — identifies me permanently, and it is printed on my identity card without being issued by it: the card carries the number, the tax agency owns it.

My instinct to guard these numbers came from growing up in the US. The Social Security number is the closest thing an American has to a unique identifier. It was never intended to be, though. From 1946 to 1972, the card itself carried "NOT FOR IDENTIFICATION." Chris Hibbert, who maintained a long-running [SSN FAQ](https://web.archive.org/web/20240803013548/http://cpsr.org/issues/privacy/ssn-faq/), [told The Verge in 2012](https://www.theverge.com/2012/9/26/3384416/social-security-numbers-national-ID-identity-theft-nstic): "There has long been a confusion over whether the SSN is a secret (and so would be useful for verifying someone's identity) or a public identifier (and thus should be treated as well known)."

What makes the NIF and the codice fiscale safe to hand to anyone is that each is a username and nothing more — public from the start, the codice fiscale computable from a person's own details, the NIF verifiable by its check digit. No second credential is universally held in the US, so "knows the number" became proof of identity by default. The Social Security number is a username too, but it has to serve as the password as well: a shared secret that never changes, is the same at every institution, and is routinely accepted over the phone. Every American knows the "last 4 of your social" question. That's the actual flaw: one credential doing two jobs it was never built for.

The first Social Security numbers were issued in 1936. For most of this number's life, there was no way for a bank to check that the person giving it was the person it belonged to. That finally changed in 2020, when the Social Security Administration launched [eCBSV](https://www.ssa.gov/dataexchange/eCBSV/), a verification service Congress had to legislate into existence in 2018 to fight synthetic identity fraud. The API arrived eighty-four years after that first number, and it answers exactly one question: whether a name, a date of birth, and a number match SSA's records. That confirms the number belongs to that name. It does not confirm that the person in front of you *is* that name. There is still no general-purpose way to check who a number belongs to, just purpose-built ones — eCBSV for banks, SSNVS for employers reporting wages, SSOLV for states issuing driver's licenses.

Two other systems go further than any of these. E-Verify, the check that confirms a new hire is allowed to work, puts a government-held photo in front of the employer — the exact thing eCBSV cannot do. That photo check only fires on a federal document: a green card, an Employment Authorization Document, a US passport or passport card. SAVE does something similar for state agencies confirming someone's immigration status. Both hang off a federally issued document rather than the Social Security number. That is why a citizen carrying nothing but a state driver's license never triggers the photo match, and never meets SAVE at all. Everyone outside these purpose-built systems validates by inference instead: credit reports, background checks, commercial identity verification.

Something that looks like a canonical record does exist. SSA's NUMIDENT file has held a name, a date of birth, and a place of birth for nearly every citizen since enumeration at birth began in the late 1980s. What it has never had is a general-purpose read path — a way for another institution to use it as a canonical identity lookup — and a record no one else can point at isn't a canonical record.

There is clear value in having a federal-level identifier for an individual person. Non-citizens get a purpose-built one: the Department of Homeland Security assigns an A-number, short for alien registration number. Unlike the Social Security number, it was built to be an identifier and nothing else — it follows a person through every change of status, and onto the naturalization certificate if they become a citizen. For years it reached only part of that population: a green card holder had one, a tourist or a student typically didn't. That gap closed in April 2025. A [DHS rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/29/2026-13057/alien-registration-form-and-evidence-of-registration) finalized in June 2026 requires effectively every non-citizen aged fourteen or over who stays thirty days to register, be fingerprinted, and be issued a number if they don't already have one.

## What proves it: the credential

For most American citizens, the answer to identity verification is a driver's license — the one document most likely to tie together their identity and their residency. Since the ability to drive is effectively required to function in most of the US, most people have one. Federal ID is the exception rather than the rule — a passport, a passport card, a military ID — and since many Americans don't travel outside the country, they don't bother to acquire a passport.

European identity systems make an identification document an explicit part of civic life, where identity is the entire point of the document — not health, not driving, not anything else. For Italy, that is the Carta d'Identità Elettronica (CIE). It is governed by two EU instruments: [Directive 2004/38/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2004/38/oj/eng) requires every member state to issue its nationals an identity card or a passport, and requires the other member states to accept it, but says nothing about what that document looks like. Uniformity came later, with [Regulation (EU) 2019/1157](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2019/1157/oj/eng), which defines common security standards for national identity cards — struck down by the Court of Justice in 2024 over the procedure used to adopt it rather than its substance, and re-enacted as [Regulation (EU) 2025/1208](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2025/1208/oj/eng).

The card is not yet how most Italians actually log in to public services — that is still SPID, a credential issued independently of any identity document. Retiring SPID has been floated repeatedly and the government has pointed the transition at the CIE and the IT Wallet, but in October 2025 AgID renewed the identity-provider agreements through 2027 instead — the consolidation is under way and unfinished. The CIE carries my unique identifier, the codice fiscale, and an NFC chip that authenticates me directly. Since the physical card has to be present, it satisfies the security requirement: the codice fiscale is my username, the CIE is my password. As SPID recedes, that pairing becomes how most Italians will authenticate. Italy is collapsing its credentials back onto a single document — normalization playing out in real time.

Portugal implements its own version of this. Citizens receive a Cartão de Cidadão. The rest of us use a passport or residence title tied to us. Either way the credential stays separate from the document: most commonly the [Chave Móvel Digital](https://www.autenticacao.gov.pt/a-chave-movel-digital), though the Cartão de Cidadão's own certificates and an eIDAS credential from another member state authenticate just as well.

The US license has to prove both identity and residency at once, and it falls apart very quickly on the second. People move and keep the old license until it expires.

US non-citizens, meanwhile, have a very different situation. United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) issues several federal photo IDs. The permanent resident card (Form I-551, known colloquially as a green card) is the obvious example, and the Employment Authorization Document (Form I-766) is another. 8 U.S.C. § 1304(e) requires any registered non-citizen 18 or older to carry their registration document at all times, and failing to do so is a misdemeanor. No US citizen has a comparable duty to carry one. So the country that supposedly has no ID card does issue one — and only mandates carrying it for the people who are not citizens.

## Where you live: the register

For me, as an Italian citizen legally residing in an EU member state (Portugal) for studies, signing a lease was only the first half of arriving. Free movement gives any EU citizen an unconditional right to be in Portugal for three months; past that, you are required to register your presence with the municipality you live in, within thirty days of the three-month mark.

The document that records it is the Certificado de Registo de Cidadão da União Europeia (CRUE), Portugal's implementation of [Directive 2004/38/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2004/38/oj/eng) — the same instrument that requires Italy to issue me an identity card also establishes my right to move to Portugal and live here. One directive, answering two of the three questions.

Obtaining it requires a specific list of documents. I arrived at the office with my Italian passport, proof of financial means, proof of enrollment at university, and proof of my residential address. I received the certificate immediately, and I watched the data go into a government system accessible to anyone who needs to validate it. I can go to a [website](https://check.aima.gov.pt/doc/Verificacao.aspx) run by the Portuguese immigration authority Agência para a Integração, Migrações e Asilo (AIMA), enter the access code printed on the document (free to share), and confirm that it's legitimate.

My wife on the other hand is not an EU citizen, so her pathway has a different set of frictions. Being married to an EU citizen still gets her an expedited process, but she has to go through AIMA directly — slower, and more of it left to interpretation. The route is nothing like mine. The destination is: the same three questions, answered in the same normalized structure, on the same join key.

That differs sharply from the US, where I could move to New York and, unless I wanted a New York driver's license, go a long time without telling anyone I lived there, right up until I had to file a New York tax return. The US produces many answers to the question "where do you live," each authoritative to the institution holding it. If you last filed a federal tax return living in Illinois, the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) has that address until you file again, or submit a specific IRS change of address request. The Social Security Administration may have a different one. All of them are authoritative, and none is a single source of truth.

Unless, again, you're an immigrant! Form AR-11 requires a change of address within 10 days of moving (8 U.S.C. § 1305), and failing to file is a criminal offense; for students and exchange visitors, the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System (SEVIS) does the same job, with the school reporting on their behalf. So the mandatory federal residence register exists, but only for foreigners.

## When you leave

Italy keeps track even after you leave. Once I acquired Italian citizenship from abroad, I was enrolled as part of that process in a system called the Anagrafe degli Italiani Residenti all'Estero (AIRE) — a registry of every Italian citizen living abroad. It is a declaration rather than an automatic consequence of citizenship; an Italian who moves abroad later has to file it themselves. As a citizen registered in AIRE, I deal with the consular district covering my residence for passports, registering life events, voting, and the rest.

Having just moved abroad myself, I can say there is no US equivalent to AIRE for citizens. Instead, the US keeps track of citizens living abroad indirectly, and far more invasively, through the tax code. The US is one of only three countries that tax on the basis of citizenship at all — Eritrea levies a flat 2% on its diaspora, and Myanmar has taxed nonresident citizens' foreign salary income since 2023 — and it is the only one that applies its full domestic income tax code to citizens wherever they live.

Where that differs from a formalized system like AIRE is that the IRS knows I live abroad, but until I specifically tell anyone else, they don't. Worse, plenty of US institutions will simply stop doing business with you once you aren't residing there, which, if your stay abroad is temporary, becomes a real problem. It is another patchwork built on the fragmented federal and state delineation that makes the US function, often enough, as "50 mini countries under a trenchcoat."

Unless, once more, you're not a citizen! For a non-citizen, leaving is itself a filing: a permanent resident who gives up the status submits Form I-407, and USCIS is required by law to report it to the IRS. Until then the registration obligations keep running, and they are backed by criminal statute rather than by a tax bill.

## The normalized identity database

What I actually took from registering in Portugal was the shape of it. My Italian identity document, the CRUE, and the NIF are not three separate copies of me across multiple databases. Logically, they're three tables — one answering who I am, one proving it, and one recording where I live. That is a lens I brought with me rather than a schema any of these states sat down and wrote, but it holds all the way through. That's what normalization means in a database: you don't duplicate a fact, you point at the one place it lives, so there is never a second copy to go quietly out of date.

Italy comes closest of the three. The register is the ANPR (Anagrafe Nazionale della Popolazione Residente), owned by the Ministry of the Interior, and a change of residence is *one* write — online, through the [ANPR portal](https://www.anpr.interno.it/). Everything downstream still keeps its own records, so this is replication rather than a true foreign key, but every copy is fed from that single write. It is a first step toward what the EU calls the "once-only principle": you tell the state something once, and the state carries it from there.

Portugal comes close in that the data is the right shape. The NIF acts as a stable join key, and every administrative domain has an authoritative owner, but those systems don't share a common canonical record. It's essentially a normalized schema where the foreign keys aren't entirely connected, so updating my address would still have to be done in several places. It falls short in other ways too: the document list can be left up to interpretation, and the registration itself doesn't cross EU borders, so moving to another member state means starting over. [eIDAS](https://eidas.ec.europa.eu/efda/home) is the piece of this I have actually leaned on: a credential issued by one member state authenticates to another's services, with nothing re-issued. Authentication travels; registration doesn't.

The US is denormalized for its citizens. My address is *copied* into every institution that needs it, each an independent write, with nothing propagating between them. Copies drift. So a loose collection of proxies stands in for the structure, and every time I answered that question in New York I assembled it out of the same three or four documents. The stale address on that New York driver's license isn't an administrative failure; it's an update anomaly, the exact thing normalization exists to prevent.

## Who it was built for

The US does have a normalized identity database — canonical record, stable join key, one authoritative owner, mandatory propagation on change — it just lives in the immigration system. It is nearly the mirror image of Italy: the propagation fires at the boundaries, registration, status change, exit, rather than running continuously under every fact the way ANPR does. A non-citizen gets the join key and the propagation, but is billed for it in duties and obligations that no citizen carries, nor any of the potential benefits. For its own citizens, the country has refused to build this type of population register.

<CitizenAsymmetry />

[REAL ID](https://www.tsa.gov/real-id) has been enforced in the US since May 2025, and it is a genuine step: a state now has to check an applicant's Social Security number against SSA records rather than take the card at face value. But look at what each applicant gets checked against: a citizen's status rests on a birth certificate or a passport, a non-citizen's on a live federal database called SAVE. Even here, the same split. What REAL ID standardizes is how the states issue their own documents; it doesn't build the thing underneath them, at least not for citizens.

The same pattern shows up at the airport. Getting into TSA PreCheck or Global Entry means an application, a fee, a background check, fingerprints, and an in-person interview. A citizen who wants a faster identity check has to volunteer for a small version of the registration regime non-citizens are compelled into, and pay for it, in five-year increments. Turning up without a compliant ID at all costs less and buys only a few days of it, with no promise that it works. My EU passport gets me the same thing at the border, for free. There is no application because there is nothing to apply for: the chip authenticates against a record that already exists — the codice fiscale and the CIE again, username and password. In one system, trust in a citizen is manufactured one traveler at a time and sold. In the other, it is a property of the record.

The US built a population register and pointed it at people who cannot vote. The one place it points where people can vote? Selective Service: a mandatory federal registration of male, 18-25 year old citizens, with criminal penalties for not registering. And it is about to get the propagation as well. Section 535 of the [FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/PLAW-119publ60), signed in December 2025, makes that registration automatic from December 2026 — Selective Service assembling the roll itself out of records other agencies already hold, rather than waiting to be told. So the machinery is buildable after all, and the country can build it without asking anyone to fill in a form. It just gets built for conscription: pure obligation, with no read path back to the person.

I walked through the EU citizen door in Portugal and had a certificate the same afternoon. I walked through the citizen door in America and got a filing cabinet. Same person, two paths, and the path is what determined the schema I experienced. None of that is something I earned.

An immigrant in Portugal would have a different process to build their database, but once it is built, it would look much like mine. An immigrant in the US would have neither my process nor my end result: far more is recorded about them at the federal level than any US citizen is required to hold.

The Privacy Act of 1974 stopped government agencies from denying you a benefit for refusing the Social Security number — with exceptions wide enough to cover tax and driver's licensing — and said nothing at all about anyone else, which is why a landlord, a bank, and a doctor's office can all still insist. Something as straightforward as REAL ID took two decades to overcome state resistance.

The US has tried the alternative once already. The 2011 [National Strategy for Trusted Identities in Cyberspace](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/default/files/rss_viewer/NSTICstrategy_041511.pdf) set out to grow a market of private credentials on the explicit condition that participation stay voluntary and that the federal government "use existing private-sector Identity Ecosystem solutions rather than developing or operating its own" — the register ruled out in the founding document. Fifteen years on, the Social Security number is still what a bank asks for.

I'll be curious whether these systems ever start talking to each other in earnest. The EU is trying — there's a digital identity wallet coming that would carry all of this across borders, and I'll sign up the day it ships.

It is worth knowing what that machinery has already survived. When the identity-card regulation reached the Court of Justice in 2024, the fingerprints in my own CIE were upheld as proportionate — and the regulation was struck down anyway, because mandating biometrics put the regulation under a treaty article requiring unanimity in the Council, not the majority procedure the EU had used. Not a court refusing to let the register be built. A court naming the price of building it.

The US could stand to take a page out of the European book, but needs to do so in a way that properly protects the civil liberties of the population. That objection is why it was never built, and it has already failed on its own terms: the register got built anyway, and pointed at the people with the least protection from it.

Refusing to build one for citizens never prevented the surveillance; it only withheld the half that helps. Without those protections, it'll end up a surveillance state for citizens and immigrants both, and nothing more. Normalization is not politically neutral.
