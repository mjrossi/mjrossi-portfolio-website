America decided that a free country does not keep a register of its people — and then built one for the people who cannot vote. I only saw the shape of it after moving to Portugal as an Italian citizen and being handed, in a single afternoon, the thing my own country has spent ninety years refusing to build.

## Who it was built for

The US does have something much closer to a normalized identity database than anything it maintains for its own citizens. It just lives in the immigration system. Four pieces have to be present for that claim to mean anything, and there they all are: a canonical record, one authoritative entry per person; a join key, one identifier every other system can refer to; a single owner answerable for each fact; and mandatory propagation, a legal duty to report a change so that the record follows the person. A non-citizen gets the join key and the propagation, but they come wrapped in duties and obligations no citizen carries, without equivalent benefits. For its own citizens, the country has refused to build this type of population register.

I walked through the EU citizen door in Portugal and had a certificate the same afternoon. I walked through the citizen door in America and got a filing cabinet: a birth certificate from one county, a Social Security card from one agency, a driver's license from one state, and nothing joining them. Same person, two paths, and the path is what decided how much of me each country bothered to write down. None of that is something I earned.

An immigrant in Portugal would go through a different process to establish their record, but once established, it would look much like mine. An immigrant in the US would have neither my process nor my end result: far more is recorded about them at the federal level than any US citizen is required to hold.

That table isn't a bureaucratic accident. The four empty cells on the citizen side aren't a computer's decision — computers don't have opinions about who gets a column. Congress and DHS do, and a schema is a record of who a state decided to know.

## What a system needs

As an engineer, I recently worked on an identity and authorization system. From day one, I knew I would need two key pieces: a canonical record and a single source of truth.

Two definitions, in full this time: a canonical record is one entry. In this case, that entry is the resident. The resident has an identifier stable enough that every other system can point at it. A single source of truth means there is one place that owns a specific fact about that resident, and every other copy is fed from that source. If two systems disagree, the source of truth settles which is correct. That's also what normalization means in a database: you don't duplicate a fact, you point at the one place it lives, so there is never a second copy that can quietly go out of date.

By that standard the US has neither for its citizens. It keeps authoritative records organized around each agency's own purpose instead, so pulling together the specifics on a person means cross-referencing several of them: a driver's license here, a lease there, a utility bill standing in as proof that "this person exists at this address." All of it accepted as commonplace. It was startling to move into a system that has something closer to both — a canonical record and a single source of truth — albeit far from perfect. For non-citizens, though, the US comes much closer to both.

Strip the problem down and there are only three questions any identity system has to answer: who you are, what proves it, and where you live.

## Who you are: the identifier

The Portuguese Número de Identificação Fiscal (NIF) is a tax identification number. It is analogous to the US Social Security number. I went to the pet store the afternoon we arrived in Portugal, and they asked me for it. I was so confused — why would a store want this? I assumed that, like my Social Security number, it should be kept secret.

Apparently not, and the reason turns out to be the thesis of this entire post. The number is linked to Finanças, the Portuguese tax authority, and giving it at checkout files that receipt into e-fatura, the invoice record Finanças keeps against my NIF. How it counts depends on what I bought: health, education, and housing can count toward deductions from the tax I owe, while receipts from restaurants, garages, and gyms earn a deduction based on a percentage of the IVA I paid. Either way the state is holding the receipt, and it has the classification pre-filled as much as possible before I ever open my tax return — no physical shoebox of receipts to reconcile in the spring. The pet store was asking because *I* benefit.

Italy does the same, and I learned it years ago. Italian identity documents and citizen identifiers are far more formalized than the US equivalents. My codice fiscale — the Italian equivalent of a Social Security number, issued by the Agenzia delle Entrate — identifies me permanently, and it is printed on my identity card without being issued by it: the card carries the number, the tax agency owns it.

My instinct to guard these numbers came from growing up in the US. The Social Security number is the closest thing an American has to a unique identifier. It was never intended to be, though. From 1946 to 1972, the card itself displayed "NOT FOR IDENTIFICATION." Chris Hibbert, who maintained a long-running [SSN FAQ](https://web.archive.org/web/20240803013548/http://cpsr.org/issues/privacy/ssn-faq/), [told The Verge in 2012](https://www.theverge.com/2012/9/26/3384416/social-security-numbers-national-ID-identity-theft-nstic): "There has long been a confusion over whether the SSN is a secret (and so would be useful for verifying someone's identity) or a public identifier (and thus should be treated as well known)."

The NIF and the codice fiscale are designed as identifiers rather than authenticators — public from the start, the codice fiscale computable from a person's own details, the NIF verifiable by its check digit. No second credential is universally held in the US, so "knows the number" became proof of identity by default. The Social Security number is a username too, but it has to serve as the password as well: a shared secret that never changes, is the same at every institution, and is routinely accepted over the phone. Every American knows the "last 4 of your social" question. That's the actual flaw: one credential doing two jobs it was never built for.

The first Social Security numbers were issued in 1936. A direct check arrived eighty-four years later, when the Social Security Administration launched [eCBSV](https://www.ssa.gov/dataexchange/eCBSV/) for banks in 2020. It answers one narrow question: whether a name, date of birth, and number match SSA's records. That verifies the record, not the person presenting it. The other verification paths are purpose-built too — SSNVS for employers and SSOLV for states issuing driver's licenses.

E-Verify and SAVE go further because they hang off federal documents rather than the Social Security number. E-Verify can put a government-held photo in front of an employer when a new hire presents a green card, Employment Authorization Document, US passport, or passport card; SAVE lets state agencies check immigration status. A citizen carrying only a state driver's license triggers neither E-Verify's photo matching nor SAVE. SSA does hold something like a canonical record in its NUMIDENT file, but there is no general read path that lets other institutions use it as a national identity lookup.

There is clear value in having a federal-level identifier for an individual person. Non-citizens who pass through USCIS get a purpose-built one: the Department of Homeland Security assigns an A-number, short for alien registration number. Unlike the Social Security number, it was built to be an identifier and nothing else — it follows a person through every change of status, and onto the naturalization certificate if they become a citizen.

The duty to register is much older, dating to 1940, and many visitors and students already satisfy it through a visa or Form I-94 without receiving an A-number. What changed in April 2025 was more mundane: DHS created Form G-325R so people missed by the existing forms finally had a way to comply. The [rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/29/2026-13057/alien-registration-form-and-evidence-of-registration), finalized in June 2026, did not create the duty. The registration system was already there; the new form gave it a path to nearly everyone.

## What proves it: the credential

For most American citizens, the answer to identity verification is a driver's license — the one document most likely to tie together their identity and their residency. Since the ability to drive is effectively required to function in most of the US, most people have one. Federal ID is the exception rather than the rule — a passport, a passport card, a military ID — and since many Americans don't travel outside the country, they don't bother to acquire a passport.

European identity systems make an identification document an explicit part of civic life, where identity is the entire point of the document — not health, not driving, not anything else. For Italy, that is the Carta d'Identità Elettronica (CIE). It is governed by two EU instruments: [Directive 2004/38/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2004/38/oj/eng) requires every member state to issue its nationals an identity card or a passport, and requires the other member states to accept it, but says nothing about what that document looks like. Uniformity came later, with [Regulation (EU) 2019/1157](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2019/1157/oj/eng), which defines common security standards for national identity cards — struck down by the Court of Justice in 2024 and re-enacted as [Regulation (EU) 2025/1208](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2025/1208/oj/eng).

The card is not yet how most Italians actually log in to public services — that is still SPID, a credential issued independently of any identity document. The government has pointed the transition at the CIE and the IT Wallet, but in October 2025 AgID renewed the identity-provider agreements through 2027 instead — the consolidation is under way and unfinished. The CIE carries my unique identifier, the codice fiscale, and an NFC chip that authenticates me directly. Since the physical card has to be present, it satisfies the security requirement: the codice fiscale is my username, the CIE is my password. As SPID recedes, that pairing becomes how most Italians will authenticate. Italy is collapsing its credentials back onto a single document — normalization playing out in real time.

Portugal implements its own version of this. Citizens receive a Cartão de Cidadão. The rest of us use a passport or residence title tied to us. Either way the credential stays separate from the document: most commonly the [Chave Móvel Digital](https://www.autenticacao.gov.pt/a-chave-movel-digital), though the Cartão de Cidadão's own certificates and an eIDAS credential from another member state authenticate just as well.

The US license has to prove both identity and residency at once, and it falls apart very quickly on the second. People move and keep the old license until it expires.

US non-citizens, meanwhile, have a very different situation. United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) issues several federal photo IDs. The permanent resident card (Form I-551, known colloquially as a green card) is the obvious example, and the Employment Authorization Document (Form I-766) is another.

8 U.S.C. § 1304(e) requires any registered non-citizen 18 or older to carry their registration document at all times, and failing to do so is a misdemeanor. No US citizen has a comparable duty to carry one. So the country that supposedly has no ID card does issue one — and only mandates carrying it for the people who are not citizens.

## Where you live: the register

As an Italian citizen studying and legally residing in Portugal, signing a lease was only the first half of arriving. Free movement gives any EU citizen an unconditional right to be in Portugal for three months; past that, you are required to register your presence with the municipality you live in, within thirty days of the three-month mark.

The document that records it is the Certificado de Registo de Cidadão da União Europeia (CRUE), Portugal's implementation of [Directive 2004/38/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2004/38/oj/eng) — the same instrument that requires Italy to issue me an identity card also establishes my right to move to Portugal and live here. One directive, answering two of the three questions: what proves who I am, and where I live.

Obtaining it requires a specific list of documents. I arrived at the office with my Italian passport, proof of financial means, proof of enrollment at university, and proof of my residential address. I received the certificate immediately, and I watched the data go into a government system accessible to anyone who needs to validate it. I can go to a [website](https://check.aima.gov.pt/doc/Verificacao.aspx) run by the Portuguese immigration authority Agência para a Integração, Migrações e Asilo (AIMA), enter the access code printed on the document (free to share), and confirm that it's legitimate.

My wife, on the other hand, is not an EU citizen, so her pathway has a different set of frictions. Being married to an EU citizen still gets her an expedited process, but she has to go through AIMA directly — slower, and more of it left to interpretation. The route is nothing like mine. The destination is the same normalized structure, answering the same three questions on the same join key.

That differs sharply from the US, where I could move to New York and, unless I wanted a New York driver's license, go a long time without telling anyone I lived there, right up until I had to file a New York tax return. The US produces many answers to the question "where do you live," each authoritative to the institution holding it. If you last filed a federal tax return living in Illinois, the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) has that address until you file again, or submit a specific IRS change of address request. The Social Security Administration may have a different one. All of them are authoritative, and none is a single source of truth.

Unless, again, you're an immigrant — or, in the language of US law, even a nonimmigrant! Form AR-11 requires a change of address within 10 days of moving (8 U.S.C. § 1305), and failing to file is a criminal offense; for students and exchange visitors, the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System (SEVIS) does the same job, with the school reporting on their behalf. So the mandatory federal residence register exists, but only for foreigners.

## When you leave

The three questions leave one thing unanswered: what happens once you're gone. Italy keeps track even after you leave. Once I acquired Italian citizenship from abroad, I was enrolled as part of that process in a system called the Anagrafe degli Italiani Residenti all'Estero (AIRE) — a registry of every Italian citizen living abroad. It is a declaration rather than an automatic consequence of citizenship; an Italian who moves abroad later has to file it themselves. As a citizen registered in AIRE, I deal with the consular district covering my residence for passports, registering life events, voting, and the rest.

There is no US equivalent to AIRE for citizens. Instead, the US keeps track of citizens living abroad indirectly, and far more invasively, through the tax code. The US is among the very few countries that tax on the basis of citizenship at all — Eritrea levies a flat 2% on its diaspora, and Myanmar has taxed nonresident citizens' foreign salary income since 2023 — and is distinctive in applying its full domestic income tax code to citizens wherever they live.

Where that differs from a formalized system like AIRE is that the IRS knows I live abroad, but until I specifically tell anyone else, they don't. It is another patchwork produced by the division of authority between the federal government and the states, making the US function, often enough, as "50 mini countries under a trenchcoat."

Unless, once more, you're not a citizen! For a non-citizen, leaving is itself a filing: a permanent resident who gives up the status submits Form I-407, and USCIS is required by law to report it to the IRS. Until then the registration obligations keep running, and they are backed by criminal statute rather than by a tax bill.

## The normalized identity database

What I actually took from registering in Portugal was the shape of it: my Italian identity document, the CRUE, and the NIF are not three separate copies of me across multiple databases, but three tables sharing one key. That is a lens I brought with me rather than a schema any of these states sat down and wrote, but it holds all the way through.

Italy comes closest of the three. The register is the ANPR (Anagrafe Nazionale della Popolazione Residente), owned by the Ministry of the Interior, and a change of residence is *one* write — online, through the [ANPR portal](https://www.anpr.interno.it/). Everything downstream still keeps its own records, so this is replication rather than a true foreign key, but each one can be fed from that single write. It is a first step toward what the EU calls the "once-only principle": you tell the state something once, and the state carries it from there.

Portugal comes close in that the data is the right shape. The NIF acts as a stable join key, and every administrative domain has an authoritative owner, but those systems don't share a common canonical record. It's essentially a normalized schema where the foreign keys aren't entirely connected, so updating my address would still have to be done in several places. It falls short in other ways too: the document list can be left up to interpretation, and the registration itself doesn't cross EU borders, so moving to another member state means starting over. [eIDAS](https://eidas.ec.europa.eu/efda/home) is the piece I have actually leaned on: a credential issued by one member state authenticates to another's services, with nothing re-issued. Authentication travels; registration doesn't.

The US is denormalized for its citizens. My address is *copied* into every institution that needs it, each an independent write, with nothing propagating between them. Copies drift. The stale address on that New York driver's license isn't an administrative failure; it's an update anomaly, the exact thing normalization exists to prevent.

The immigration system is nearly the mirror image of Italy: propagation fires at specific boundaries — registration, status change, and exit — rather than carrying every changed fact continuously through the system.

## What got built instead

The US refusal to build a federal population register is usually framed as a civil-liberties choice: keep identity decentralized, leave documents to the states, and deny the federal government a master roll of its citizens. But decentralization did not prevent surveillance. It outsourced the read path to credit reports, background checks, and commercial identity-verification services, while leaving citizens with no propagation between public systems and few of the benefits a register could confer.

Every alternative anyone did put forward preserved the same rule: the federal government would not operate a general identity system, while private institutions remained free to keep asking for the Social Security number. The Privacy Act of 1974 stopped government agencies from denying you a benefit for refusing the number — with exceptions wide enough to cover tax and driver's licensing — but said nothing about landlords, banks, or doctors. The 2011 [National Strategy for Trusted Identities in Cyberspace](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/default/files/rss_viewer/NSTICstrategy_041511.pdf) tried to grow a market of private credentials on the explicit condition that the federal government use private-sector solutions rather than operate its own. Fifteen years on, the Social Security number is still what a bank asks for.

[REAL ID](https://www.tsa.gov/real-id) has been enforced in the US since May 2025, and it is a genuine step: a state now has to check an applicant's Social Security number against SSA records rather than take the card at face value. But a citizen's status still rests on a birth certificate or passport, while a non-citizen's is checked against a live federal database called SAVE. REAL ID standardizes how states issue their documents; it does not build the thing underneath them, at least not for citizens.

The contrast is clearest when I enter each country. Returning to the US as a citizen without Global Entry means a manual immigration queue. To make that experience frictionless, I have to apply, pay a fee, undergo a background check, give fingerprints, sit for an interview, and repeat the process every five years. Entering the EU as an EU citizen, I present my chip-enabled passport at an electronic gate. There is no separate trusted-traveler program to join because the passport authenticates against a record that already exists. In one system, trust in a citizen is manufactured one traveler at a time and sold. In the other, it is a property of the record.

The US built a population register and pointed it at people who cannot vote. The one place it points where people can vote? Selective Service: a mandatory federal registration of male citizens ages 18 through 25, with criminal penalties for not registering. And it is about to get the propagation as well. Section 535 of the [FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/PLAW-119publ60), signed in December 2025, makes that registration automatic beginning in December 2026 — Selective Service assembling the roll itself out of records other agencies already hold, rather than waiting to be told. So the machinery is buildable after all, and the country can build it without asking anyone to fill in a form. It just gets built for conscription: pure obligation, with no read path back to the person.

## The price of building it right

I'll be curious whether these systems ever start talking to each other in earnest. The EU is trying — member states are required to provide digital identity wallets by the end of 2026, and I'll sign up the day mine ships.

It is worth knowing what that machinery has already survived. When the identity-card regulation reached the Court of Justice in 2024, the fingerprints in my own CIE were upheld as proportionate — and the regulation was struck down anyway, because mandating biometrics put the regulation under a treaty article requiring unanimity in the Council, not the majority procedure the EU had used. Not a court refusing to let the register be built. A court naming the price of building it.

The US could stand to take a page out of the European book, but only with real protection for civil liberties. That concern is one reason a citizen register was never built. But the refusal has already failed on its own terms: the surveillance emerged anyway, aimed first at the people with the least protection from it.

Refusing to build one for citizens never prevented the surveillance; it only withheld the half that helps. Without those protections, building one now would only finish the surveillance state — for citizens and immigrants both. Normalization is not politically neutral.
