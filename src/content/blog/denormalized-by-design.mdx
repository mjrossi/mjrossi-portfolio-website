I walked through the EU citizen door in Portugal and left with a certificate. I walked through the citizen door in America and got a filing cabinet: a birth certificate from one county, a Social Security card from one agency, a driver's license from one state, and nothing joining them.

Same person, two paths, and the path is what decided how much of me each country bothered to write down. None of that is a reward for anything I did. What stayed with me was not simply that the Portuguese government knew more about me. It was that the system gave something back for what it took: one identifier that follows me, records that agree with each other, and a credential that proves I am the person those records name.

## Who it was built for

That machinery is one identifier that follows you, records that agree with each other, and a credential behind them. The US has built much more of it inside the immigration system than it maintains for its own citizens. Across different immigration statuses, the federal government can assign a lasting identifier, record an arrival, require an address change, follow a change of status, and record a departure. No single non-citizen passes through every one of those systems: a visitor, a student, and a permanent resident travel different lanes. Taken together, though, the pieces form a federal recordkeeping architecture around non-citizens that has no citizen equivalent.

That difference matters because the same machinery can be a service or a threat, depending on what it is for. Citizens get duplicated paperwork, a fragile Social Security number, and private companies assembling identity records for sale. Non-citizens get more connected federal records, but mainly through duties backed by penalties. One group gets the costs of decentralization; the other gets the costs of coordination. Neither gets a public identity system designed first around the person using it.

The empty cells on the citizen side aren't a computer's decision — computers don't have opinions about who gets a column. ## What a system needs

As an engineer, I recently worked on an identity and authorization system, and the first question I had to answer is the one a government has to answer too.

Say the state needs two facts about me, my name and where I live, for two purposes: taxing me, and licensing me to drive. The obvious way to store that is to let each purpose keep what it needs.

| Purpose | Name | Address |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tax | Matthew Rossi | Lisboa, Portugal |
| Driving | Matthew Rossi | Brooklyn, New York |

Both rows were correct the day they were written. Then I moved, and I told the tax office, because tax is the one that comes looking. Nobody told the DMV. Neither row is a lie and neither is marked stale — each is authoritative to the office holding it, and nothing here says which one describes where I actually sleep. Store one fact in two places and you have signed up to change it in two places, forever. Databases call the result an update anomaly.

Store it the other way and my address has exactly one home:

| Person | Name | Address |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Matthew Rossi | Lisboa, Portugal |

with everything that needs it holding a pointer instead of a copy:

| Purpose | Person |
| --- | --- |
| Tax | 1 |
| Driving | 1 |

Moving now means changing one row, and both purposes see it, because neither of them ever had its own version to forget about. That is most of what normalization means: a canonical record — one authoritative entry per person, carrying an identifier stable enough that everything else can point at it — and a single source of truth for each fact it holds, so no second copy exists to drift.

By that standard the US has neither for its citizens. Each agency keeps the record it needs for its own purpose, so proving the specifics of one person means cross-referencing a driver's license here, a lease there, and a utility bill standing in as proof that "this person exists at this address" — all of it accepted as commonplace. It was startling to move into a system that comes closer to joining those facts, albeit far from perfectly.

Strip the problem down and there are only three questions any identity system has to answer: who you are, what proves it, and where you live.

## Who you are: the identifier

The Italian codice fiscale is a tax identification number. This number is intended to be sharable knowledge — so sharable, in fact, that the heuristics for calculating it are so explicitly defined that [online calculators exist](https://codicefiscale.expert/en) to determine what yours would be, should you request it from the Agenzia delle Entrate. Italian identity documents and citizen identifiers are far more formalized than the US equivalents. My codice fiscale identifies me permanently, and my identity card prints it without issuing it: the card carries the number, the tax agency owns it.

The Portuguese Número de Identificação Fiscal (NIF) is the Portuguese tax identification number, analogous to the Italian codice fiscale. It is also not intended to be a secret, but that was unknown to me the day we arrived in Portugal and went to the pet store. They asked me for it, and I was extremely confused — why would a store want this? I assumed that this code was like my US Social Security number, and that it should be kept secret in all but the most important communications.

Apparently not, and the reason turns out to be the thesis of this entire post. The number is linked to Finanças, the Portuguese tax authority, and giving it at checkout files that receipt into e-fatura, the invoice record Finanças keeps against my NIF. How it counts depends on what I bought: health, education, and housing can count toward deductions from the tax I owe, while receipts from restaurants, garages, and gyms earn a deduction based on a percentage of the value-added tax (IVA) I paid. Either way the state is holding the receipt, and it has the classification pre-filled as much as possible before I ever open my tax return — no physical shoebox of receipts to reconcile in the spring. The pet store was asking because *I* benefit.

My instinct to guard these numbers came from growing up in the US. The Social Security number is the closest thing an American has to a unique identifier. It was never intended to be, though. From 1946 to 1972, the card itself displayed "NOT FOR IDENTIFICATION." Chris Hibbert, who maintained a long-running [SSN FAQ](https://web.archive.org/web/20240803013548/http://cpsr.org/issues/privacy/ssn-faq/), [told The Verge in 2012](https://www.theverge.com/2012/9/26/3384416/social-security-numbers-national-ID-identity-theft-nstic): "There has long been a confusion over whether the SSN is a secret (and so would be useful for verifying someone's identity) or a public identifier (and thus should be treated as well known)."

The NIF and the codice fiscale are designed as identifiers rather than authenticators — public from the start, the codice fiscale computable from a person's own details, the NIF verifiable by its check digit. No second credential is universally held in the US, so "knows the number" became proof of identity by default. The Social Security number is a username too, but it has to serve as the password as well: a shared secret that never changes, is the same at every institution, and is routinely accepted over the phone. Every American knows the "last 4 of your social" question. That's the actual flaw: one credential doing two jobs it was never built for.

The first Social Security numbers were issued in 1936. A direct check arrived eighty-four years later, when the Social Security Administration launched [eCBSV](https://www.ssa.gov/dataexchange/eCBSV/) for banks in 2020. It answers one narrow question: whether a name, date of birth, and number match SSA's records. That verifies the record, not the person presenting it. Employers and state agencies get other narrow checks, but there is still no general public credential behind the number and no read path that lets an institution verify a citizen against a federal identity record.

Parts of the immigration system go further because they hang off federal documents. E-Verify can put a government-held photo in front of an employer when a new hire presents one of several federal documents; the Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements (SAVE) system lets state agencies check immigration status. A citizen carrying only a state driver's license triggers neither E-Verify's photo matching nor SAVE.

Many people who pass through United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) also receive a purpose-built identifier: the A-number, short for alien registration number. It follows a person through changes of status and onto the naturalization certificate if they become a citizen. It is not universal. Many visitors and students satisfy the registration requirement through a visa or Form I-94 without receiving one. In April 2025, DHS created Form G-325R so people missed by the existing forms finally had a way to comply with a duty dating to 1940. The [final rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/29/2026-13057/alien-registration-form-and-evidence-of-registration) that followed in June 2026 did not create a single new lane for every non-citizen; it closed gaps in a registration system assembled across many of them.

## What proves it: the credential

European identity systems make identification an explicit part of civic life. For Italy, that is the Carta d'Identità Elettronica (CIE), a national card built to common EU security standards. It carries my codice fiscale and a near-field communication (NFC) chip that can authenticate me directly. Since the physical card has to be present, the pairing does what the Social Security number cannot: the codice fiscale is the username, and the CIE is the password.

Most Italians still use the Sistema Pubblico di Identità Digitale (SPID), a separately issued digital credential, to log in to public services. The government is gradually moving those logins toward the CIE and the IT Wallet, but the transition is unfinished. That matters less here than the direction of travel: Italy has an identifier and a credential designed as two different things.

Portugal implements its own version of this. Citizens receive a Cartão de Cidadão. The rest of us use a passport or residence title tied to us. Either way the credential stays separate from the document: most commonly the [Chave Móvel Digital](https://www.autenticacao.gov.pt/a-chave-movel-digital), though the Cartão de Cidadão's own certificates do the same, as does a credential from another member state issued under eIDAS, the EU regulation on electronic identification and trust services.

For most American citizens, the answer to identity verification is a driver's license — the one document most likely to tie together their identity and their residency. Since the ability to drive is effectively required to function in most of the US, most people have one. Federal ID is the exception rather than the rule — a passport, a passport card, a military ID — and since many Americans don't travel outside the country, they don't bother to acquire a passport.

The US license has to prove both identity and residency at once, and it falls apart very quickly on the second. People move and keep the old license until it expires.

<CredentialSplit />

US non-citizens, meanwhile, have a very different situation. USCIS issues several federal photo IDs. The permanent resident card (Form I-551, known colloquially as a green card) is the obvious example, and the Employment Authorization Document (Form I-766) is another.

8 U.S.C. § 1304(e) requires any registered non-citizen 18 or older to carry their registration document at all times, and failing to do so is a misdemeanor. No US citizen has a comparable duty to carry one. So the country with no national ID card does issue one — and only mandates carrying it for the people who are not citizens.

## Where you live: the register

As an Italian citizen studying and legally residing in Portugal, signing a lease was only the first half of arriving. Free movement gives any EU citizen an unconditional right to be in Portugal for three months; past that, you are required to register your presence with the municipality you live in, within thirty days of the three-month mark. You register with the municipality, not with Portugal, and that is the level where the answer gets used. How many people a city believes live on a given street is the input to how often a bus runs down it, whether the school stays open, and whether the road gets designed for the people on it or the cars crossing it. I treated mine as an errand with a deadline. It is also the number the city plans from.

The document that records it is the Certificado de Registo de Cidadão da União Europeia (CRUE), Portugal's implementation of [Directive 2004/38/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2004/38/oj/eng) — the instrument that requires Italy to issue me an identity card or a passport also establishes my right to move to Portugal and live here. One directive, answering two of the three questions: what proves who I am, and where I live.

Obtaining it requires a specific list of documents. I arrived at the office with my Italian passport, proof of financial means, proof of enrollment at university, and proof of my residential address. I received the certificate immediately, and I watched the data go into a government system accessible to anyone who needs to validate it. I can go to a [website](https://check.aima.gov.pt/doc/Verificacao.aspx) run by the Portuguese immigration authority Agência para a Integração, Migrações e Asilo (AIMA), enter the access code printed on the document (free to share), and confirm that it's legitimate.

My wife, on the other hand, is not an EU citizen, so her pathway has a different set of frictions. Being married to an EU citizen still gets her an expedited process, but she has to go through AIMA directly — slower, and more of it left to interpretation. The route is nothing like mine. The destination still connects her identity, the document that proves it, and the place where she lives.

That differs sharply from the US, where I could move to New York and, unless I wanted a New York driver's license, go a long time without telling anyone I lived there, right up until I had to file a New York tax return. The US produces many answers to the question "where do you live"; each is authoritative to the institution holding it. If you last filed a federal tax return living in Illinois, the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) has that address until you file again, or submit a specific IRS change of address request. The Social Security Administration may have a different one. All of them are authoritative, and none is a single source of truth.

Unless, again, you're an immigrant — or, in the language of US law, even a nonimmigrant. Form AR-11 requires a change of address within 10 days of moving (8 U.S.C. § 1305), and failing to file is a criminal offense; for students and exchange visitors, the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System (SEVIS) does the same job, with the school reporting on their behalf. So mandatory federal address reporting exists for registered non-citizens, but not for citizens.

The three questions leave one thing unanswered: what happens once you're gone. Italy keeps track even after you leave. It maintains a separate register for citizens abroad, the Anagrafe degli Italiani Residenti all'Estero (AIRE). Because I acquired Italian citizenship while already living abroad, I was enrolled as part of that process. My consular district now handles my passports, life events, and voting.

There is no US equivalent to AIRE for citizens. The IRS knows I live abroad because the US taxes citizens wherever they live, but no general record carries that fact to the rest of government. A permanent resident who gives up that status, by contrast, can file Form I-407, and USCIS reports the departure to the IRS. That is another connection built for a specific immigration consequence, not a service offered to everyone who leaves.

## What one change costs

Changing an address is where the architecture stops being abstract. In Italy, a resident writes the change once to the Anagrafe Nazionale della Popolazione Residente (ANPR), the national population register, and downstream agencies can receive it from there. This is replication rather than a true foreign key, but it moves toward what the EU calls the "once-only principle": tell the state something once, and the state carries it from there.

Portugal has a stable key in the NIF and an authoritative owner for each administrative domain, but has no single resident record connecting all of them. Updating my address still means telling Finanças, the municipality, AIMA, and anyone else separately. The country has some of the right pieces without one place that joins them.

The US citizen has neither the shared record nor the key connecting the copies. My address is written independently at the DMV, the IRS, Social Security, my bank, and my utility company. My New York driver's license still carries an address I no longer live at — an update anomaly, exactly the ordinary failure that normalization is meant to prevent.

Taken together, the immigration systems are nearly the mirror image of Italy: propagation fires at specific boundaries — registration, status change, and exit — instead of carrying every changed fact continuously. It is not one normalized database. It is a set of federal systems made to connect when the government decides the connection matters.

## What got built instead

The absence of a federal population register in the US is usually framed as a civil-liberties choice: keep identity decentralized, leave documents to the states, and deny the federal government a master roll of its citizens. But decentralization did not prevent surveillance. It outsourced the read path to credit reports, background checks, and commercial identity-verification services, while leaving citizens with no propagation between public systems and few of the benefits a register could confer.

Every alternative put forward preserved the same rule: the federal government would not operate a general identity system, while private institutions remained free to keep asking for the Social Security number. The Privacy Act of 1974 limited when government agencies could demand one, with wide exceptions, while landlords, banks, and doctors kept asking. [REAL ID](https://www.tsa.gov/real-id), passed in 2005 and enforced from May 2025, standardized how states issue their documents but still left the underlying citizen record fragmented. The 2011 [National Strategy for Trusted Identities in Cyberspace](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/default/files/rss_viewer/NSTICstrategy_041511.pdf) tried to grow a private market for better credentials on the explicit condition that the federal government not operate one itself. Fifteen years on, the Social Security number is still what a bank asks for.

The contrast is clearest when I enter each country. Returning to the US as a citizen without Global Entry means a manual immigration queue. To make that experience frictionless, I have to apply, pay a fee, undergo a background check, give fingerprints, sit for an interview, and pay to renew every five years. Entering the EU as an EU citizen, I present my chip-enabled passport at an electronic gate. There is no separate trusted-traveler program to join because the credential and the citizen record are already part of the ordinary border system. In one system, trust in a citizen is manufactured one traveler at a time and sold. In the other, it is a property of the record.

One federal register does reach citizens. Selective Service registers men ages 18 through 25, citizens and non-citizens alike, with criminal penalties for not registering — and beginning in December 2026 it will populate itself. Section 535 of the [FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/PLAW-119publ60), signed in December 2025, makes registration automatic, with Selective Service assembling the roll from records other agencies already hold rather than waiting to be told. That is the propagation this entire post has described the absence of: one write, carried across agencies, with no form for anyone to file. So the machinery is buildable after all, and the country can build it without asking a single citizen for anything. It gets built for conscription: pure obligation, with no read path back to the person.

## The price of building it right

The US should build a federal civic identity system that works for everyone who lives here, citizens and non-citizens alike. I don't mean a master dossier of where everyone goes and what everyone does, I mean a narrow public utility that consists of one durable identity, a secure credential that proves who holds it, and a small set of core facts about a person that can be corrected once and used across public services. The federal government should be able to deliver a benefit, issue a document, or update an address without making an ordinary person carry the mismatch between its databases.

The limits are the system, not promises made after it exists. The law names the few facts the record may hold and the purposes for which each may be read; anything unnamed is not storable. An agency asking whether a person is over eighteen gets that answer, not the date of birth behind it. Nothing logs where the credential was presented, so there is no itinerary to subpoena. The identifier never doubles as an authenticator — that is the Social Security number's failure, written as a rule — and using a public service creates no commercial identity trail.

A person can read their own record, correct it, and appeal a refusal, with no more effort than the state spent writing it. Commercial access is prohibited unless the individual grants it. Law enforcement comes through specific legal process, never a general identity lookup. Independent oversight audits both the software and the agencies using it. A biometric used to issue a credential stays on the card, never becomes a searchable population database, and is deleted from the enrollment system once the credential ships.

The objection to all of this is that a connected record becomes a condition of being served — that when the credential fails, the person disappears from the service, and it fails first for the people with the least standing to argue. That is the real failure mode, and it is not hypothetical to me. My wife's route through AIMA is the same architecture as mine with more of it left to interpretation, and every point left to interpretation is a point where someone gets to decide about her. A system like this is only as good as what it does for the person in the slower lane. That is what the constraints are for: not to make the record smaller, but to make it answerable to the person it describes.

The EU is not a clean model, but it shows that these constraints can be written into the machinery. Member states are required to provide digital identity wallets by the end of 2026, with selective disclosure and control left to the person using one. I'll sign up the day mine ships. The [identity-card regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2025/1208/oj/eng) provides no basis for a centralized EU biometric database, restricts who may read fingerprints, and requires the biometric data used to produce a card to be erased within ninety days. Those rules still need enforcement, and they will be tested. That's what democratic control looks like: not assuming the state will behave, but deciding in law and architecture what it is allowed to do.

The same protections should cover citizens and non-citizens. A connected record should not exist only where the government wants an obligation enforced, and civil liberty should not belong only to people who can vote. I want a government capable enough to make rights and services portable, and a system constrained enough that the record can never become an itinerary.

Refusing to build a public system never prevented the surveillance; it privatized much of it and withheld the useful utility. Build one without these protections, though, and all we have done is finish the surveillance state for citizens and immigrants both. Normalization is not politically neutral.
