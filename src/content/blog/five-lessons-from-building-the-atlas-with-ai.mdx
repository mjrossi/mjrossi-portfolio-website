Over the last two posts I wrote about the thought process behind the Urbanist Atlas: [the code](/blog/i-used-ai-to-build-the-urbanist-atlas/) — the Go API and the React frontend — and then [the data](/blog/the-atlas-data-was-the-hard-part/), where modeling cities and verifying organizations turned out to be the harder half.

This is the third and final part, where I step away from the technical and get to what I actually learned.

## Where AI fell short

There were a good number of instances where I had to ask myself: is this really saving me time? There are a lot of loops, a lot of time spent waiting, and reviewing output over and over again often becomes fairly mind-numbing.

Some of that was the models. Some of it was the workflow they created. There's an art and a craft to writing software, and I found that some of it gets harder to access when you're spending your time reviewing generated code instead of building it yourself. The code these models produce can often be just average; something trained on a vast corpus of existing code tends toward the familiar, not necessarily the best solution for your particular problem.

Despite all the human review I built into the process, not writing the code myself made it hard to fully and accurately know what I was working with. When I write code, the act of writing it forces me to construct a mental model in real time. When AI writes it, I have to construct that mental model through review. It feels analogous to handwriting vs. typing — handwriting is going to do a better job of committing something to memory.

And the end result isn't the only thing that has to be right. The security parameters need to be fleshed out. The deploy strategy has to be robust and handle intermittent failure. The internal structure of the software, such as how you're importing and using third-party libraries, or building out abstractions across the network, database, and hardware layers — all of this matters.

The AI can do it. It's probably good enough, and it does work. But is it _really_ the best for _your_ specific problem? That's hard to answer with AI-assisted coding like this.

### The energy and environmental cost

I paid for one month of a Claude Max subscription to do this. That's $100. To say nothing of the fact that many people can't afford that sort of spend, there are a lot of tokens spent on each subagent, each feature, each AI-assisted review cycle or organizational pass. Data centers are expanding rapidly to meet increased demand. In my home state of Michigan, at least thirteen data centers are in various planning stages, and [residents are organizing against them](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/americans-are-angry-about-data-centers-politicians-are-feeling-pressure-2026-07-16/). They cite impacts on their towns' water supply, power grid, and rural character.

I did make some deliberate choices not to reach for the biggest hammer by default. Opus was my primary model, but simpler subagent work — a documentation pass, a formatting cleanup, a first read of a diff — went to Haiku or Sonnet instead. I tried to keep agents few and tightly scoped rather than spawning a swarm just because I could, and to batch review passes instead of re-running them every time I changed my mind.

I had this in the back of my mind when writing the Atlas, and I admit it's a hard trade-off that I didn't have a good answer to. The concept of tokens often feels really abstract, so I can't piece together how compute-intensive these tasks are. I want to be honest about the limits of that, though: I never measured any of it. I can't tell you what it saved, because I don't know. It was restraint by instinct rather than by instrumentation, which is a weaker answer than the question deserves.

## Five lessons from building with AI

Having walked through the actual work — the code, the data, where it fell short — here are the five takeaways I keep coming back to for using AI effectively. They all come back to the same thing: you have to know enough about the problem to disagree with the answer.

### Be *very* explicit with your input

There is a strong difference between "I want to build a directory of safe street organizations" and what I actually used as my starting point, which was something more like this:

"We are going to build a directory of safe-streets and urban advocacy organizations. I want this to be a modular application that can be consumed by a number of consumers. To start, let's build this in three pieces, starting with a simple API. This API will be built in Go. You should default to using standard libraries wherever possible, and ask before importing third party dependencies. Build it with a focus on TDD. Plan a skeleton of what this will look like."

Every clause in there is doing work. TDD is test-driven development — writing tests alongside, or in the strictest version before, the implementation — and asking for it up front meant tests existed from the first commit instead of being bolted on months later, when nobody wants to write them. Naming the standard library as the default is what kept the dependency list short. None of that happens if the prompt is one sentence long, and the model was never going to volunteer it.

That prompt names Go, and the first post covered how it got there: I left the stack open, Claude suggested Node, and I turned it down because I had specific reasons — static typing, a straightforward concurrency model, a language I'd shipped a production application in recently. So being explicit improves the model's output, but the bigger gain is doing that thinking first — it gives you something concrete to hold the model's answer against. If you can't say why you want something, you'll take whatever you're handed.

### Never take the first result

I'll admit that "never" is a strong word here. However, it was pretty rare that I was fully satisfied with the first result that the AI would return to me. It might "work," but perhaps the code wasn't structured well. There were some edge cases or tests missing. Or I realized there would be a better abstraction I could pull out somewhere.

I did multiple passes of simply "look for refactor opportunities" or "look for simplifications" — or, if I spotted a specific issue, I'd just tell the AI what I wanted. This is where our job as software engineers shifts a little bit from writing the code to architecting it. That's the ground the old acronyms cover — SOLID, YAGNI ("you aren't gonna need it") — and they mostly boil down to building the thing you actually need, and building it so the next person can change it without fear. Claude doesn't always land there on its own, so you say so (see point one: be explicit with your input!)

The region graph from the last post is the clearest case of this. I seeded that brainstorming session with New York, Chicago, and San Francisco specifically because I knew those three places well enough to push back on a bad answer, and the multi-parent graph is what came out of the pushing. Tiers are the obvious way to model where somewhere is: a city inside a county inside a metro inside a state. They're also wrong, in a way you only catch if you already know that Park Slope has no border to look up and that Chicagoland crosses a state line.

That's iteration: working the same problem with the same tool until the answer is good. It's necessary. But everything inside that loop is still being judged by the thing that produced it, which is what the next lesson is for.

### Review, review, and then review again

Fresh context is what does the real work here. An agent that just spent an hour building something is carrying every assumption it made along the way, and it'll usually grade its work against those assumptions instead of against reality.

Once a plan was drafted, I reviewed it and pushed back. Finally, after plenty of back and forth, I let it build. After it was built, I threw another agent with a fresh context to review the code that was generated. After 2 or 3 rounds of this, I'd do my own code review, leaving feedback, doing manual security analysis, and then throw another agent on it to address the feedback.

The polygon function from the last post is why I kept doing this even when it felt like overkill. That code ran clean, returned a number, and the number was entirely plausible — a few postal codes just quietly resolved to the wrong metro. No amount of iterating would have surfaced that, because there was nothing there to look wrong. What catches a bug like that is something with no stake in the answer being right: a fresh agent, a deterministic regeneration that diffs against what's committed, or a person reading it slowly.

There are open-source extensions for Claude Code that formalize this kind of loop, so you don't have to hold the whole process in your head. I used [Superpowers](https://github.com/obra/superpowers), which packages workflows like brainstorming, planning, and code review into skills the agent reaches for on its own. The specific tool matters less than the shape of it — small, focused scopes, and a review done by something that didn't write the code in the first place.

The last two lessons are less about how to work the tool and more about what you shouldn't hand it, however well the first three are going.

### Remember what AI actually is

These models are trained on an enormous body of existing human work — the whole reason they're any good. That tells you *where* to aim the reviewing from the last two lessons.

A system like that is strongest where your problem is ordinary, because ordinary is what it learned from. It's weakest where your problem is strange. Every hard thing in this project sat at the strange end: a ZIP code whose neighborhood has no legal boundary, two federal datasets that disagree about what year it is, a polygon with a hole in it. Claude handled the ordinary parts of the Atlas faster and more thoroughly than I would have alone. The parts that were specific to *this* project are where I had to slow down.

So the practical version is: before you start reviewing, ask which parts of what you're building are unusual. That's your list, and that's where it'll be confidently, plausibly wrong.

### Know what only you can decide

The fact is that these are machines with limitations. It's important not to anthropomorphize them: they can make mistakes, just like the humans whose work they learned from.

The mistakes aren't really the point, though. The six inclusion criteria from the last post are — local focus, active advocacy, transit or safe streets, no government agencies, no consultancies, no housing.

No training corpus was going to tell me that housing and YIMBY work, a neighboring movement and a friendly one, sits outside the Atlas's scope. Or that a group other people still write about, but that hasn't posted in two years, isn't one I can confidently send someone to. Those are decisions about what the directory is for and who it owes something to. A model can apply criteria like that very well once they exist; someone has to have chosen them, and be willing to defend the choice.

There's a reason I think about Data from Star Trek: The Next Generation. Data is an extremely capable artificial life-form — he can execute nearly anything asked of him — but the character is compelling because of what he can't originate: wanting things, valuing things, choosing what matters.

## If you want to try something like this

It's never been easier to take an idea to a working prototype. That's an incredibly powerful tool, and the hope is that we'll see great ideas come to life that couldn't before. If you want to build your own idea, I hope this series was useful.

I'd be wary of anyone handing you a process to copy, mine included. The specific shape of this project came out of what I already had — thirteen years of backend work, almost no frontend, and a subject I care about enough to argue with a model about. Yours will come out of something different. The real lesson is knowing what you want well enough to argue about it, not stopping at the first thing that works, and getting something other than the author to check it. Where you know the domain, I'd delegate hard and review fast. Where you don't, slow down — or the AI's plausible answer will get expensive to untangle.

Beyond the model choices I mentioned earlier, the tooling is all there to borrow. Since the code is open source, I'd encourage you to look at my `just` file if you want to take any of it — like the containerized link validation that checks every organization's website through a VPN and flags the dead ones.

## Why I'm being transparent about AI

That kind of borrowing is only possible because everything is openly licensed. The code, data, and written content are all publicly available under the [Apache 2.0](https://github.com/mjrossi/urbanist-atlas/blob/main/LICENSE), [ODbL-1.0](https://github.com/mjrossi/urbanist-atlas/blob/main/LICENSE-DATA) and [CC-BY-SA-4.0](https://github.com/mjrossi/urbanist-atlas/blob/main/LICENSE-CONTENT) licenses, respectively. I'm not seeking monetary compensation for the project, aside from a small tip jar. I really encourage folks not to throw coins into it unless they really want to and have already thrown some coins into the jar of their local advocacy orgs first.

I encourage people to reach out, submit corrections, tell me about a new advocacy org they'd like included. Trust has to be a core value, and trust is built from being open and transparent. This project is a love letter to all my fellow advocates who want to make their cities the best places they can be. Hiding behind a robot, or not being forthcoming about the fact that I am using AI to build, operate, and maintain this site, would erode the trust that I know people are placing in me.

AI is going to reshape a lot of how we work in the coming years, and there are powerful incentives pushing that in the wrong direction — maximizing profit even when the costs are borne by people and the planet. I've met some incredible activists over the years, and have been inspired by their work and passion for their homes. The Urbanist Atlas is my small way of doing my part — building something that helps people make their cities a little better.

Looking back, nearly every place it succeeded or failed came down to what I handed off and what I kept.
