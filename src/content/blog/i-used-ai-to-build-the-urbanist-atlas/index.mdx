This post has been stewing for the last month, in-between all of the aspects that go into an international move (which could be its own post someday) — my wife Hilary and I have relocated to Lisbon, Portugal, in preparation for my first year in the EIT Urban Mobility Master's program (Sustainable Urban Mobility Transitions) at Instituto Superior Técnico.

[The Urbanist Atlas](https://urbanistatlas.com) — a directory of local safe-streets and transit advocacy organizations, searchable by ZIP or postal code — has been live for about six weeks now, which feels like enough time to reflect on what worked, what didn't, and where I still had to step in instead of letting Claude run on its own. That six weeks of reflection became a two-part series: this first post is the technical one, walking through how I used Claude Code as my "coding assistant" while greenfielding the project. The second post picks up where this one leaves off, with tips and broader, non-technical thoughts about AI.

## Upfront disclaimers

Yes, I vibe coded a substantial amount of the Atlas. But "vibe coding" has gotten so overused it's practically lost its meaning, and loaded enough to make people roll their eyes, so it's worth saying what I actually mean by it: AI generated a lot of code, but I was the one specifying architecture, reviewing the generated changes, writing and running tests, validating the data, and making the calls when the AI's first answer wasn't the right one. I'm neither an AI evangelist nor an AI doomer — I try to recognize where AI is helpful (repetitive tasks, extra analysis), and where we should be concerned (energy and environmental impacts).

"OK, so what are you writing here?" would be a valid question to ask.

In a social media ecosystem filled with absolutes and binaries, the intention of this post is to be upfront about how I used Claude Code as a tool to help me write the Atlas: where AI was helpful and effective, where there were shortcomings, and hopefully an example of where a modern software engineer can use AI, but where that AI is only as effective as the engineer behind the keyboard.

## AI for the code

### The Go API

I'm primarily a backend engineer, so I've designed my fair share of APIs, build tools, low-level data layers, integrations, etc. This is where I could be the most picky and explicit about how I wanted the code built. It was also my natural starting point, since I wanted the API to drive everything, including the inevitable frontend, and future ability to allow users to directly integrate with the API.

I knew I wanted Go, but I also wanted to see how Claude would fare, so I began a brainstorming session. My prompt was explicit about what I wanted, but not about the tech stack — at first. I directed Claude to build an API that would support the ability to both power a frontend, but also be directly accessible by `curl`. I started with the simplest endpoint, `/lookup`, since I could use that as a complete slice of a working bit of functionality. I didn't want to worry about the data quite yet, so I directed Claude to hard-code `11217` (my zip code) with a single result: one advocacy organization, the kind of entry the Atlas is built to catalog.

It suggested that I write it all in Node / JavaScript. I disagreed. That was partially my preference, but I also had specific technical reasons: I wanted static typing, a straightforward concurrency model, and, practically, a language I'd already written a robust production application in recently. Node would have worked fine here too; that's not the point. The point is I had reasons, and I could articulate them. To me, this was the first trap that can burn folks who don't have a software engineering background when they try to vibe code: the danger isn't that AI will make a bad choice, it's that it'll make a perfectly plausible one you don't know enough to question.

At this stage, Claude started proposing a plan for Go. There was a lot of back and forth; I was explicit in my input. From this one endpoint, we needed to manage the initialization logic, the application configuration, and how and where it was going to be deployed, etc. Could I have just taken its recommendation? Yes. But after thirteen years of writing code, and very recently writing an extremely robust application in Go, I had my own ideas of how I wanted to do this, so I stayed just as explicit with Claude about the rest. Rather than hand all of that — initialization, configuration, deployment — to a single pass, I acted as a bit of a project manager, product owner, and software architect in one go, and split the work across three of Claude Code's subagents: one focused purely on the endpoint and API design, another on deployment and tooling (the `just` file and `mise` config that show up below), and a third whose only job was to review the first two against idiomatic Go and Go community convention.

And Claude delivered! By being extremely detailed in my input, leveraging my knowledge, we ended up with an endpoint and structure of code I was pleased with. Over time, Claude and I added more layers, more endpoints, a `just` file and `mise` for managing tooling. If I was unclear or didn't have a strong preference, there was the back and forth, and I'd explicitly push the AI to look at community guidelines on making good choices.

Another area where the human matters — those community guidelines exist because humans who care about good code quality are out there, and publish their work.

### The React SPA

This is where I knew I was going to run into more difficulty. I _don't_ have strong frontend experience. My JavaScript knowledge at the start of this application is basically "jQuery is definitely not a thing anymore, right?". Beyond a few small edits to larger frontend framework applications, it was not a strong suit for me.

The frontend was therefore a more interesting test of my thesis: I was delegating more of the implementation and design decisions precisely because I knew less.

I'm not a designer either, so I used [Claude Design](https://claude.com/product/design) — Anthropic's dedicated design product — to draft the look of the frontend before writing any code or deciding on a framework. I'd already done an iteration of this on my personal blog and website, so I used that as a starting point, responding to each draft, asking for a different layout or visual hierarchy, and iterating until the handoff document matched what I actually wanted. I was also impressed that Claude Design could produce handoff materials for Claude Code to go ahead and implement the frontend code, which I definitely took advantage of.

As for the frontend itself, I was direct in my initial prompts — I have a lot of experience as a backend engineer, minimal frontend, but have dabbled in HTML, CSS, JavaScript, TypeScript, Node, etc. I leaned much more heavily on Claude's input here, but was explicit that I needed advice, not just answers. I knew my high-level requirements: easy to maintain; no stored state, consuming everything from the API; hosted on Cloudflare; thoroughly tested; and open to community input. Claude provided a couple of solutions, but ultimately I went with the tried and true React solution.

From here, I built the first iteration of the UI that consumed that single `/lookup` endpoint from before. As with the backend, this single slice was a deliverable and something that showcased a working prototype that could be iterated on over time. And as before, Claude delivered: a real page that called the live endpoint and rendered a real result, not a mockup. I now had a monorepo with two separate but connected pieces: a backend API in Go, and a frontend app in React consuming that Go API. Hurrah!

### Fleshing out more features

Thinking like a product manager was really valuable here, because I've seen how useful it can be to just ship something that works. It doesn't need all the features ready. It doesn't need to be perfect. It just needs to prove the basic structure holds up. This did.

From that point on, I could work on the backend and frontend in parallel, using each side to validate the other. Add a new endpoint with a frontend to consume, ensure it worked. Make changes on the frontend, know that the backend was stable. I directed Claude to write an OpenAPI spec, so we were working with hard contracts that future agents — and me, and other contributors — could rely on. I always wanted to ensure that at the end of the day, nobody was going to feel stuck if they didn't have an AI robot to lean on to look at, review, or contribute to this code. To that end, specific documentation passes, tooling passes, thorough and well-written `just` commands, were always done in between new feature work or bugfixes.

Eventually it got to the point where we needed real data to work with.

## AI for the data

### The region graph

Cities are complex, and their boundaries aren't always static — which is exactly the kind of complexity the Atlas needed to model to connect a postal code to the right advocacy groups. This was a fascinating portion of this project because it directly tied into the urban planning field, the very thing I'm pivoting my career into.

I started with the United States, where I'm from — as they say, "write what you know." I wanted ZIP codes to be the anchor for looking up advocacy groups, but learned quickly that ZIP codes are a useful starting point for a search, not themselves the editorial unit I could rely on to anchor a key data model against. I knew about Metropolitan Statistical Areas (MSAs), but advocacy is local — 11217 is Brooklyn, but it's also Park Slope, but Park Slope isn't a hard defined border. Brooklyn is NYC, but if you type in 11217, that's also part of the New York - New Jersey - Connecticut MSA. But what if you're in CT but not part of the defined NY Metro?

Just from that paragraph, you can see how complex this was going to get: I needed a way to represent geographic relationships that aren't strictly hierarchical. So I did a Claude brainstorming session. I said "let's start by modeling New York City, Chicago, and San Francisco." These are places I knew well enough that I knew the brainstorming would be valuable in defining an initial draft, but I could push back if something didn't make sense. I didn't want to just hard code — that's not going to scale. I didn't want to include national organizations as an editorial decision. So, Claude and I built a directed acyclic graph (DAG).

You can read in thorough detail [here](https://github.com/mjrossi/urbanist-atlas/blob/main/docs/region-graph.md) but for a high level summary:

The core idea is to stop thinking of a location as a fixed set of tiers — city, county, metro, state — and instead model geography as a graph. Every region is a node (a borough, a city, a metro, a state, a transit district, a multi-state coalition), and each node just declares its parents. A postal code (ZIP code in the US, postal code elsewhere) anchors to the smallest region I've curated for it, and a lookup walks up the graph from there, collecting every region above it and the advocacy orgs attached to each.

That's what makes 11217 resolve the way it does: it anchors to Park Slope, which has Brooklyn as a parent, which has both NYC and New York State as parents, which in turn has the New York-New Jersey-Connecticut metro above it. One lookup walks that whole chain and returns Park Slope groups, citywide NYC groups, and New York State groups together, in the right order — without New Jersey orgs leaking into a Brooklyn search just because they share a metro.

The same graph shape is what lets messier cases work without special-casing each one: Chicago's transit district covers six counties but only one state, and a city-state like Berlin is technically a _Land_ (German for federal state) but functions as a city. None of it is hard-coded to the US, either. Canada gets the same graph, tuned to its own geography: postal codes anchor at the FSA — the three-character Forward Sortation Area, the smallest boundary Canada publishes — matched to its CMA (Canada's rough equivalent of an MSA) by a spatial overlap of the two boundary shapes, since Canada has no county layer to lean on the way the US does. Ottawa-Gatineau, which straddles Ontario and Quebec, reuses the same cross-border shape I built for NYC and Chicago: a stateless umbrella metro with one region per province underneath it, so a Gatineau ZIP resolves to Quebec and never leaks into Ontario's results. I also modeled Portugal — Lisboa's multi-parent metro area, Madeira as an autonomous region with no NUTS-II parent — as a stress test of the graph against an admin geography that isn't US or Canadian. That data isn't live: it exists to validate the model, not (yet) to ship, and it's excluded from the API's default country list until the editorial coverage is ready. Germany's next, whenever that happens.

I ran specific agents to stress-test the model as it was being built, and that surfaced some genuinely hard edge cases. Washington, D.C. isn't a state — it's a city-state whose one metro spans three jurisdictions (D.C., Maryland, and Virginia), so it needed its own local/regional split rather than folding into a state-based hierarchy. Connecticut was a different kind of puzzle: in 2022 the Census Bureau retired the state's eight counties in favor of nine new planning regions as county-equivalents, and two of the federal datasets I was combining were different vintages of that same geography — one already keyed to the new planning regions, one still keyed to the legacy counties. That mismatch meant hundreds of Connecticut ZIP codes were falling through to the bare state node instead of resolving to their actual metro (Bridgeport-Stamford, Hartford, New Haven), until I added a reconciliation pass to bridge the two. And rural ZIP codes needed to correctly fall back to their parent state when no finer region applied.

Overall, I was extremely proud of the region graph design and this was a great example of using AI along with my own knowledge of cities to build an adaptable data model. I learned a lot working with these data sets.

### Cataloging the organizations

Cataloging 236 organizations on my own, across tens of thousands of postal codes in two countries, is the sort of task that would have taken weeks or months in the time before AI. The fact that I could do it (along with the rest of this project) in a little over a month of work is remarkable. That said, the data had to be accurate. It had to be verified.

The first bit was deciding exactly what organizations I wanted to include, so I defined a set of criteria that was at the forefront of any request to AI (full list [here](https://urbanistatlas.com/about)).

1. Geographic focus (the best advocacy happens locally)
2. Active advocacy (no inactive orgs; some activity within the last 12 months)
3. On-topic (transit, safe streets, etc.)
4. Government agencies, consultancies, and non-advocacy orgs are excluded (focus on advocacy)
5. Housing & YIMBY are excluded (tight focus)

So using the rules outlined above, here is the typical workflow for adding new organizations.

1. Prompt: "Please do a deep dive into organizations that are focused on advocating for safe, sustainable, and human-focused city design and transportation. Ensure that organizations follow the editorial rules defined. Review social media presence to determine activity. Ask me if you're unsure about a specific org."
2. Prompt (new agent): "Review all of the orgs found in the recent pass. Ensure they meet the criteria. Check the websites and social media presence for activity". Once that was done, I would review all the orgs myself, and also run a custom-built `just verify-org-urls` command that spins up an Alpine Linux container routed through a WireGuard VPN tunnel, hits every URL in the dataset, and flags anything dead, redirected off-domain, or squatted by a parked-domain page, then manually exclude any orgs whose site or social presence couldn't be verified.
3. Prompt: "Do another pass to look for orgs in `<region>`, seeing if we missed any or falsely flagged any that we should exclude based on the criteria." After this completed, I'd do another review step.

AI was genuinely good at the first half of that loop — casting a wide net and surfacing candidates I wouldn't have found on my own. It was never the authority on whether an organization actually belonged in the Atlas, though. That call, and the verification behind it, will always be mine.

### Did Claude hallucinate?

Yes, occasionally. I didn't encounter organizations that appeared to be entirely fabricated, but it did guess at URLs (that didn't exist), or provide some metadata that wasn't accurate. Fortunately, the combination of human review and subsequent agent reviews caught these. This is the distinction that mattered throughout the whole cataloging effort: AI-assisted research isn't the same thing as AI-verified research. The model was good at breadth and discovery; verification still required deterministic tooling and a human actually looking. It was also at this point in the process that I decided to introduce a submission desk: when a moderator approves a visitor-submitted correction or new org, a worker automatically opens a GitHub pull request appending it to the dataset, so I review a diff instead of a raw report.

Advocacy is human. Cities are human, and only exist as the incredible, culturally diverse places they are because of the people who live in them. This work is focused on making our cities better places to live. I never forgot that, no matter how many interactions I had with Claude.

### Closing thoughts

Cities aren't static — they're made and remade every day by the people living in them, in ways no dataset fully captures. No AI, predictive text generator, or any one individual is going to be able to fully understand the specific nuances of a city. One learns and understands a city by talking to its people, listening to their specific stories. A city is its people.

Use AI, and use it aggressively where it makes you faster. But don't let it stand in for your own eyes, ears, and the community of humans who call a city their home.
