This is the second of three posts about building [the Urbanist Atlas](https://urbanistatlas.com) with Claude Code. [The first](/blog/i-used-ai-to-build-the-urbanist-atlas/) covered the code: the Go API, the React frontend, and the moment I turned down Claude's first architectural recommendation because I could say why.

It ended on a warning. AI rarely hands you something obviously wrong. It hands you something plausible, and if you don't know the subject well enough to argue, you take it.

I expected the data to be the easy half. I've lived in three of the cities in the Atlas and spent years in the advocacy world in two of them. Knowing the places turned out to be enough to notice a wrong answer and nowhere near enough to explain one.

## The region graph

Cities are complicated and their boundaries move. The Atlas has to take a postal code and hand back the advocacy groups whose work actually covers it, so all of that has to fit into a data model. This was my favorite part of the project, because it's the subject of the career I'm moving into — after thirteen years as a backend engineer, I've just started a master's in sustainable urban mobility in Lisbon.

I modeled the United States first, because it's where I'm from and I could check the results against places I know. ZIP codes were the obvious anchor, since a ZIP code is what a visitor is going to type in. But a ZIP code is a postal delivery route, not a place anyone organizes around, so it can't be the unit the whole model hangs off.

The Census publishes a ready-made alternative: the Metropolitan Statistical Area, a city plus the counties that commute into it. For a while I assumed that was my answer. It isn't, because advocacy is more local than that. 11217 is Brooklyn, but it's also Park Slope, and Park Slope has no border you can look up anywhere. Brooklyn is New York City, but 11217 also sits inside the New York-Newark-Jersey City metro area, which the Census draws across two states. And Stamford, Connecticut is close enough to commute into Manhattan but sits outside that metro's boundary, while Hartford, two hours further up I-91, plainly isn't part of the New York region at all.

I needed a way to represent geographic relationships that aren't strictly hierarchical.

So I opened a brainstorming session with Claude: "let's start by modeling New York City, Chicago, and San Francisco." I picked those three because I've lived in or visited each of them enough to know when an answer was wrong.

I went in with two rules for myself. No hard-coding zip codes to organizations, because that doesn't scale. And no national organizations. That one was an editorial call rather than a technical one: a national group has a communications budget and will find you on its own, while the point of the Atlas is the chapter meeting eight blocks away that you'd otherwise never hear about.

The [region-graph design doc](https://github.com/mjrossi/urbanist-atlas/blob/main/docs/region-graph.md) has the thorough version of the model, but here's the high-level summary:

Claude's first proposal was a stack of tiers. A ZIP code sits inside a city (or a borough, in New York's case), and a city sits inside a state. That's a reasonable place to start. It's how an envelope is addressed, it's how most people picture the relationship, and it handles Brooklyn fine.

It breaks as soon as you push on it. What about an organization that covers a hundred zip codes? I wasn't going to build something as brute-forced as:
- [Riders Alliance](https://urbanistatlas.com/orgs/riders-alliance): 10001, 10002, ...

Or with zip code as the key:
- 10036: Riders Alliance, [Transportation Alternatives](https://urbanistatlas.com/orgs/transportation-alternatives), etc.

I needed something that could hold all the layers a real place stacks up in — neighborhood, borough, city, county, transit district, metro — and roll them up to a state or province without me enumerating every combination by hand.

So back to the drawing board. What Claude and I landed on instead was a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Every region is a node — a borough, a city, a metro, a state, a transit district, a multi-state coalition — and each node just declares its parents. A postal code anchors to the smallest region I've curated for it, and a lookup then *walks* the graph from there, stepping from the anchor up to its parents, then to theirs, collecting the advocacy orgs attached to every region it passes through.

Take my own ZIP code. Park Slope has no boundary I could curate against, so 11217 anchors at Brooklyn, the smallest unit around it with a real border. Brooklyn declares two parents: New York City and New York State. New York City's own parent is the New York metro, and above that sits the Tri-State Region. New York State has no parent at all. So Brooklyn reaches the state by its own direct edge instead of climbing there through the metro, and that asymmetry is the whole trick.

A lookup for 11217 returns Brooklyn groups, then citywide New York City groups, then New York State groups. On the way up it also collects the regional organizations sitting above them — [TransitCenter](https://urbanistatlas.com/orgs/transitcenter) at the metro tier, and the [Tri-State Transportation Campaign](https://urbanistatlas.com/orgs/tri-state-transportation-campaign) above that.

That last part is correct, not a bug that quietly widens the search. The Tri-State Transportation Campaign's remit also covers New Jersey and Connecticut, and if you're searching from Brooklyn it's a group you should know about. What a Brooklyn search doesn't return is New Jersey's *own* advocacy groups. [Bike JC](https://urbanistatlas.com/orgs/bike-jc), the [New Jersey Bike & Walk Coalition](https://urbanistatlas.com/orgs/new-jersey-bike-walk-coalition) and [Trenton Cycling Revolution](https://urbanistatlas.com/orgs/trenton-cycling-revolution) hang off the New Jersey state node, which is not an ancestor of Brooklyn, so they never surface.

Tri-State in, Bike JC out. If I'd parented New York State under the metro instead, both would come back, and you only catch that by naming real organizations and asking whether you'd actually want them in the results.

The same shape handles messier cases without special-casing any of them. Chicago is the clearest example of why tiers refuse to line up. Chicagoland spills across the state line into northwest Indiana, so the Census metro area is officially an Illinois-Indiana one.

The RTA, the transit district that actually funds the trains and buses there, stops dead at the Illinois border: Cook County plus five collar counties and nothing else. I only knew that boundary existed because I spent years in Chicago's advocacy world. Two different shapes over roughly the same place, so they became two different nodes instead of one blurred tier. (Illinois has since folded the RTA into the new Northern Illinois Transit Authority and the Atlas data still says RTA, which is its own small lesson about the year a dataset was frozen in.)

A search from downtown Chicago picks up both nodes. A search from Gary, Indiana picks up the metro and Indiana's groups but never the RTA and never Illinois, which is right, because Gary sits inside the metro but outside the RTA's service area and its governance.

None of it is hard-coded to the US, either. Canada gets the same graph, tuned to its own geography. There's no county layer to lean on, so postal codes anchor at the FSA (Forward Sortation Area, the smallest postal geography Canada publishes boundaries for), and each FSA is matched to the CMA (census metropolitan area, Canada's rough equivalent of an MSA) whose boundary it overlaps most — unless I've curated a city underneath that CMA, which takes precedence. Ottawa-Gatineau, straddling Ontario and Quebec, just reuses the cross-border shape I'd already built for NYC and Chicago.

Portugal I modeled as a stress test, against an administrative geography nobody designed with North America in mind, and because I was about to move to Lisbon and wanted to understand it. The model held. Lisbon's metro area has two parents for the same structural reason Brooklyn does, and Madeira needs the reverse: as an autonomous region it sits parallel to that tier rather than beneath it, with no parent at all. None of it is live, though. The seed sits in the repo next to the US and Canadian ones, but the running API doesn't load it and won't until the editorial coverage is there.

I'm proud of the region graph. But designing the shape was only half the job. It still had to be *built*, out of real government data rather than a few hand-picked cities I happened to know.

## Generating the graph

The region graph isn't hand-written. Underneath it is a pipeline that reads six staged upstream files and generates every data file the API ships with. Four are American: the US Census Bureau's metro delineation list, two crosswalks from the 2020 census tying ZCTAs (ZIP Code Tabulation Areas, the Census's statistical stand-in for a ZIP code) to counties and to places, and the Department of Housing and Urban Development's ZIP-to-county file. Two are Canadian, both Statistics Canada boundary shapefiles. One command per country rebuilds all of it: 393 US metros and 41 Canadian ones, 530 rows once the cross-border portions are counted separately, plus the anchors for over 40,000 postal codes.

I was insistent on one property here. The generator had to be deterministic — same source data in, byte-identical files out — which is what makes the data reviewable. When a regenerated file comes back with a diff (a line-by-line comparison of what it used to say against what it says now), the upstream data actually changed. A check regenerates the region files and fails if the result doesn't match what's committed, so nobody, me included, can quietly hand-edit generated data and have it survive.

This is how I ended up staring at Connecticut. I'd been asking everyone I knew in other cities to hammer on early versions, the fastest way I had to vet the model against places I couldn't check myself.

So when I was out at lunch with some of my wife's extended family, who live up in Stamford, CT, I asked for their ZIP code and tried it. It rolled up to the State of Connecticut and stopped there, when I would have expected Tri-State orgs to show up too. Stamford is 45 minutes from Grand Central Terminal and you're trying to tell me this isn't part of the NYC Metro region? Something was wrong, so I went digging.

Two of the federal datasets I was combining turned out to be different vintages of the same geography: snapshots of the same map taken years apart, each describing Connecticut's counties as they stood on the day it was published. The Census Bureau had swapped the state's eight counties for nine new planning regions in 2022, and only one of the two files had caught up. Neither was wrong. They just disagreed about what year it was.

The join between them returned nothing at all, which is the worst possible outcome, because if you didn't know any better the model looked like it was working — it just returned the state. In reality, hundreds of ZIP codes sat at the state line instead of in their actual metro, and they'd have stayed there if I hadn't gone looking. Writing the pass to reconcile the two vintages didn't take long; once I understood the problem I could articulate it to Claude and get a fix deployed. No amount of AI review was ever going to catch it, though.

That was only half of it. The Stamford ZIPs now landed in their own metro, Bridgeport-Stamford-Danbury, and still surfaced no Tri-State orgs, because nothing in that metro's record pointed up at the Tri-State region. I gave it that parent, and it's the only Connecticut metro that has one. Hartford, New Haven, Waterbury and Norwich all stop at the state. It's the same line the New Jersey groups are on, drawn this time inside a single state: Fairfield County commutes into Manhattan, and the rest of Connecticut, quite reasonably, does not consider itself part of the New York region.

The Canadian side broke differently. Matching each FSA to the CMA it overlaps most, the way I described above, sounds like a direct lookup but is really a measurement: clip the FSA's boundary against the metro's, then measure how much shape is left over. A library does the clipping. Nothing in it measures the area, so that part I had to write.

The problem sounds simple: take a clipped shape and work out how much area it contains. The catch is that a shape like that isn't one outline. It's several — an outer boundary, anything punched out of the middle of it like a lake or a river, and any islands sitting inside those. Add them all up and you get the wrong answer, because the holes need to come off the total rather than onto it. So something has to decide which outlines are holes.

The tempting shortcut is to read the direction each outline was drawn in, since a hole is conventionally drawn the opposite way round from the boundary it sits in. Do that and the holes subtract themselves, and you never have to work out which outline was which.

That's the assumption that breaks, because the clipping library doesn't honor that convention. It hands back outlines in whatever order it traced them, each drawn in whichever direction the algorithm happened to go, so a hole can easily come back drawn the same way round as the boundary around it. Its area then gets *added* to the total instead of subtracted.

Nothing crashes. Nothing throws an error. The number just comes back too big, and that number is what decides which metro an FSA belongs to.

I found it by hand. I don't know many Canadian postal codes, so I was typing in ones I'd found online for cities I knew, like Windsor, Ontario, which I used to visit when I still lived in Detroit. One of those runs came back wrong. Nothing had actually landed wrong in the shipped data up to that point, but nothing in the run would have told me if it had, and that is a much worse feeling than a failing test.

Claude and I fixed it together, by throwing out the drawing direction entirely and reading the nesting instead. The code counts how many of the other outlines each outline sits inside. An odd number means it's a hole, so its area comes off the total. An even number, including zero, means it's solid, so it gets added. Which way it was drawn never enters the calculation.

And then that fix had its own plausible-looking bug.

To ask whether one outline sits inside another, you need a point that's definitely inside the first. The obvious candidate is one of its corners, and that is exactly the wrong choice, because two outlines in a clipped shape can meet at a corner. A code-review pass over the pull request, run by a fresh agent that hadn't written any of it, caught the mistake on a test polygon: true area 9,200, measured 10,800. The hole had been counted as solid. I was already struggling to hold all of this in my head, so seeing that had me throw my hands up a bit.

The AI wrote plausible geometry code very fast, then wrote a plausible fix for it, and I signed off on both. Plausible is not the same thing as correct, and the only thing standing between the two was someone taking a deliberate second look: me on the first bug, a fresh review agent on the second.

## Cataloging the organizations

Cataloging 236 organizations across tens of thousands of postal codes in two countries could easily have taken months. AI let me do it, along with the rest of this project, in a little over a month, casting a far wider net than I could have cast alone. But the data still had to be verified.

The first step was deciding exactly what organizations I wanted to include, so I defined a set of criteria that was at the forefront of any request to AI (full list [on the About page](https://urbanistatlas.com/about)).

1. Geographic focus — a defined region, because the best advocacy happens locally
2. Active advocacy — visible public-facing work in the last 12 months, including campaigns, hearings, testimony, organizing
3. On-topic — transit advocacy or safe-streets advocacy; these are the two scopes the Atlas exists to index
4. Government agencies are excluded — such as DOTs, transit boards, and their subsidiaries
5. Consultancies are excluded — advocacy has to be the primary product, not consulting
6. Housing & YIMBY are excluded — a neighboring movement, but a tighter scope keeps local search useful

Those six rules look like an implementation detail. They're closer to the definition of the Atlas itself — the line between what belongs and what doesn't, and therefore what the whole thing is *for*. Every one of them is a call I had to make before any amount of AI research was worth anything.

In practice I hold "active" to a harder line than that list states: the evidence has to be on the organization's own site or social presence. External press alone doesn't clear the bar. A group that other people still write about but that hasn't posted in two years isn't one I can confidently send someone to.

So using the rules outlined above, here is the typical workflow for adding new organizations.

1. Prompt: "Please do a deep dive into organizations that are focused on advocating for safe, sustainable, and human-focused city design and transportation. Ensure that organizations follow the editorial rules defined. Review social media presence to determine activity. Ask me if you're unsure about a specific org."
2. Prompt (new agent): "Review all of the orgs found in the recent pass. Ensure they meet the criteria. Check the websites and social media presence for activity". Then I'd review every org myself and run a custom-built `just verify-org-urls` command, which spins up an Alpine Linux container routed through a WireGuard VPN tunnel, hits every URL in the dataset, and flags anything dead, redirected off-domain, or squatted by a parked-domain page. Anything whose site or social presence couldn't be verified came out. This is deliberately boring tooling: rather than asking an AI whether a URL looks valid, I ask a machine whether it actually resolves.
3. Prompt: "Do another pass to look for orgs in `<region>`, seeing if we missed any or falsely flagged any that we should exclude based on the criteria." Then another review step.

AI was good at the first half of that loop, surfacing candidates I wouldn't have found on my own. It was never the authority on whether an organization actually belonged in the Atlas. That call, and the verification behind it, will always be mine.

Around the same time I added a submission desk. When I approve a visitor-submitted organization, a worker automatically opens a GitHub pull request adding it to the dataset, so I review a diff instead of a raw report. Because the data is baked into the binary, it goes live on the next deployment.

## Did Claude hallucinate?

Yes, occasionally. I didn't encounter organizations that appeared to be entirely fabricated, but it did guess at URLs that didn't exist and hand me metadata that wasn't accurate, and human review and the later agent passes caught those. Which is the distinction that mattered through the whole cataloging effort: AI-assisted research isn't the same thing as AI-verified research. The model was good at breadth and discovery. Verification still took deterministic tooling and a person actually looking.

## Closing thoughts

Every one of those 236 entries is a room full of people somewhere, arguing about a bus route. I spent a month building a machine to find those rooms, and it's good at it. I found Riders Alliance the slow way, in an online thread full of New York transit obsessives, which is exactly the errand the Atlas now runs for you in about a second.

But finding them was never the hard part. Joining was: turning up to meetings, going to the gala, giving them money. No directory does that step for you, and it's the only step that has ever changed a bus route.

So use AI, and use it aggressively where it makes you faster. It made me faster. On the two occasions in this post that actually mattered — a join that silently returned nothing, a polygon that measured too big — faster is precisely what would have shipped the error.

Cities are made and remade every day by the people living in them, in ways no dataset fully captures, and the parts that matter most are usually the parts somebody has to tell you about.
