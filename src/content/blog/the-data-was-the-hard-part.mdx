This is the second of three posts about building [the Urbanist Atlas](https://urbanistatlas.com) with Claude Code. [The first](/blog/i-used-ai-to-build-the-urbanist-atlas/) covered the code — the Go API, the React frontend, and the moment I turned down Claude's first architectural recommendation because I had reasons I could articulate. It ended on a warning: the danger isn't that AI makes a bad choice, it's that it makes a plausible one you don't know enough to question.

This post is where that stopped being a warning and started costing me time. The data is where I knew the most, cared the most, and caught the most.

## The region graph

Cities are complex, and their boundaries aren't always static — which is exactly the kind of complexity the Atlas needed to model to connect a postal code to the right advocacy groups. This was a fascinating portion of this project because it directly tied into the urban planning field, the very thing I'm pivoting my career into.

I started with the United States, where I'm from — as they say, "write what you know." I wanted ZIP codes to be the anchor for looking up advocacy groups, but learned quickly that ZIP codes are a useful starting point for a search, not themselves the editorial unit I could rely on to anchor a key data model against. I knew about Metropolitan Statistical Areas (MSAs), but advocacy is local — 11217 is Brooklyn, but it's also Park Slope, and Park Slope isn't a border you can look up anywhere. Brooklyn is NYC, but 11217 also sits inside the New York-Newark-Jersey City metro area, which the Census draws across two states. And what if you're in Connecticut, close enough to feel like the New York region but outside that metro's boundary?

Just from that paragraph, you can see how complex this was going to get: I needed a way to represent geographic relationships that aren't strictly hierarchical. So I did a Claude brainstorming session. I said "let's start by modeling New York City, Chicago, and San Francisco." These are places I knew well enough that I knew the brainstorming would be valuable in defining an initial draft, but I could push back if something didn't make sense. I didn't want to just hard code — that's not going to scale. I didn't want to include national organizations as an editorial decision. So, Claude and I built a directed acyclic graph (DAG).

You can read in thorough detail [here](https://github.com/mjrossi/urbanist-atlas/blob/main/docs/region-graph.md) but for a high level summary:

The core idea is to stop thinking of a location as a fixed set of tiers — city, county, metro, state — and instead model geography as a graph. Every region is a node (a borough, a city, a metro, a state, a transit district, a multi-state coalition), and each node just declares its parents. A postal code (ZIP code in the US, postal code elsewhere) anchors to the smallest region I've curated for it, and a lookup walks up the graph from there, collecting every region above it and the advocacy orgs attached to each.

That's what makes 11217 resolve the way it does. Park Slope has no boundary I could curate against, so 11217 anchors at Brooklyn — the smallest unit around it with a real border. Brooklyn declares two parents: New York City and New York State. New York City's own parent is the New York metro, and above that sits the Tri-State Region. New York State, by contrast, is a top-level node with no parent at all.

That asymmetry is the whole trick. Brooklyn reaches New York State by its own direct edge rather than by climbing up through the metro, so a lookup for 11217 returns Brooklyn groups, citywide New York City groups, and New York State groups in that order. It also picks up the genuinely regional organizations sitting above them — TransitCenter at the metro tier, the Tri-State Transportation Campaign above that. That's correct, and worth being clear about: the Tri-State Transportation Campaign's remit really does cover New Jersey and Connecticut, and if you're searching from Brooklyn it's an organization you should know about.

What a Brooklyn search doesn't return is New Jersey's *own* advocacy groups. Bike JC, the New Jersey Bike & Walk Coalition, Trenton Cycling Revolution — those hang off the New Jersey state node, and New Jersey is not an ancestor of Brooklyn, so they never surface. That's the line the graph is drawing: a regional organization whose work genuinely covers you is worth showing, another state's neighborhood cycling group is not. Parent New York State under the metro instead, and that distinction collapses.

The same graph shape is what lets messier cases work without special-casing each one. Chicago is the clearest example of why the tiers refuse to line up. Chicagoland spills across the state line into northwest Indiana, and the Census metro area is officially an Illinois-Indiana one. But the RTA — the transit district that actually funds the trains and buses — stops dead at the Illinois border, covering Cook County plus five collar counties and nothing else. Those are two different shapes drawn over roughly the same place, so they're two different nodes rather than one fudged tier. A search from the Loop picks up both. A search from Gary, Indiana picks up the metro and Indiana's groups, but never the RTA and never Illinois — which is right, because an Indiana rider has no say in an Illinois transit agency.

None of it is hard-coded to the US, either. Canada gets the same graph, tuned to its own geography: postal codes anchor at the FSA (Forward Sortation Area, the smallest boundary Canada publishes) matched to its CMA (census metropolitan area: Canada's rough equivalent of an MSA) by a spatial overlap of the two boundary shapes, since Canada has no county layer to lean on the way the US does. Ottawa-Gatineau, which straddles Ontario and Quebec, reuses the same cross-border shape I built for NYC and Chicago: a stateless umbrella metro with one region per province underneath it, so a Gatineau postal code resolves to Quebec and never leaks into Ontario's results. I also modeled Portugal as a stress test, to see whether the graph held up against an administrative geography that nobody designed with the US or Canada in mind. Lisbon's metro area multi-parents into two separate EU statistical regions (NUTS-II, the mid-level tier the EU uses to carve up member states for reporting) — structurally the same problem as Brooklyn having two parents. Madeira needed the reverse: as an autonomous region it sits parallel to that tier rather than beneath it, with no parent at all. That data isn't live — it exists to validate the model, not to ship — and Portugal stays out of the API's default country list until the editorial coverage is ready. Germany is the next one I've sketched but haven't built. The design doc works Berlin through as a test case, a city-state that's technically a _Land_ (German for federal state) but functions like a city. Whether that holds up is a problem for whenever I get there.

I was extremely proud of the region graph design — a great example of using AI alongside my own knowledge of cities to build an adaptable data model. But designing the shape was only half the job. The graph still had to be *built*, out of real government data, and that's where the genuinely hard problems were waiting.

## Generating the graph

The region graph isn't hand-written. Underneath it is a pipeline that takes five staged upstream files — the Census Bureau's metro delineation list, two ZCTA crosswalks from the 2020 census, HUD's ZIP-to-county file, and Statistics Canada's boundary shapefiles — and generates every data file the API ships with. One command rebuilds 487 US metros, 43 Canadian ones, and the anchors for close to forty thousand postal codes.

I was insistent on one property here: the generator had to be deterministic. Same source data in, byte-identical files out. That sounds fussy, but it's what makes the data reviewable. When a diff shows up in a regenerated file, it means the upstream data actually changed — not that a map happened to iterate in a different order this time. There's a check that regenerates everything and fails if the result doesn't match what's committed, so nobody, me included, can quietly hand-edit generated data and have it survive.

Which is how Connecticut turned into a puzzle. Two of the federal datasets I was combining were different vintages of the same geography — the Census Bureau had swapped the state's counties for new planning regions in 2022, and only one of the two files had caught up. Neither was wrong. They just disagreed about what year it was, and the join between them returned nothing rather than failing loudly, stranding hundreds of ZIP codes at the state line instead of their actual metro until I wrote a pass to reconcile the two.

The Canadian side broke differently. Matching each FSA to the CMA it overlaps most, the way I described above, sounds like a lookup but isn't — it means actually computing the area of a polygon, in code, without a geospatial library. The obvious implementation is to add up the signed areas of the shape's outlines. That's wrong, and wrong in a way nothing tells you about: the library handing over those shapes makes no guarantee about which direction a hole is drawn in, so a hole's area gets *added* to the total instead of subtracted. Nothing crashes. Nothing throws an error. A handful of postal codes just quietly resolve to the wrong metro.

I caught that one, and then Claude and I fixed it together — you work out whether an outline is a hole by counting how many other outlines it sits inside. Odd means it's a hole and comes off the total; even means it's solid and gets added.

That one function is the argument of this whole post in miniature. The AI wrote plausible geometry code very fast. Plausible is not the same thing as correct, and the only thing standing between the two was a person reading it.

## Cataloging the organizations

Cataloging 236 organizations on my own, across tens of thousands of postal codes in two countries, is the sort of task that would have taken weeks or months in the time before AI. The fact that I could do it (along with the rest of this project) in a little over a month of work is remarkable. That said, the data had to be accurate. It had to be verified.

The first bit was deciding exactly what organizations I wanted to include, so I defined a set of criteria that was at the forefront of any request to AI (full list [here](https://urbanistatlas.com/about)).

1. Geographic focus — a defined region, because the best advocacy happens locally
2. Active advocacy — visible public-facing work: campaigns, hearings, testimony, organizing
3. On-topic — transit advocacy or safe-streets advocacy, the two scopes the Atlas exists to index
4. Government agencies are excluded — DOTs, transit boards, and their subsidiaries
5. Consultancies are excluded — advocacy has to be the primary product, not consulting
6. Housing & YIMBY are excluded — a neighboring movement, but a tighter scope keeps local search useful

In practice I hold "active" to a harder line than that list states: something visible on the organization's own site or social presence within the last 12 months. External press alone doesn't clear the bar. A group that other people still write about but that hasn't posted in two years isn't one I can confidently send someone to.

So using the rules outlined above, here is the typical workflow for adding new organizations.

1. Prompt: "Please do a deep dive into organizations that are focused on advocating for safe, sustainable, and human-focused city design and transportation. Ensure that organizations follow the editorial rules defined. Review social media presence to determine activity. Ask me if you're unsure about a specific org."
2. Prompt (new agent): "Review all of the orgs found in the recent pass. Ensure they meet the criteria. Check the websites and social media presence for activity". Once that was done, I would review all the orgs myself, and also run a custom-built `just verify-org-urls` command that spins up an Alpine Linux container routed through a WireGuard VPN tunnel, hits every URL in the dataset, and flags anything dead, redirected off-domain, or squatted by a parked-domain page, then manually exclude any orgs whose site or social presence couldn't be verified.
3. Prompt: "Do another pass to look for orgs in `<region>`, seeing if we missed any or falsely flagged any that we should exclude based on the criteria." After this completed, I'd do another review step.

AI was genuinely good at the first half of that loop — casting a wide net and surfacing candidates I wouldn't have found on my own. It was never the authority on whether an organization actually belonged in the Atlas, though. That call, and the verification behind it, will always be mine.

## Did Claude hallucinate?

Yes, occasionally. I didn't encounter organizations that appeared to be entirely fabricated, but it did guess at URLs (that didn't exist), or provide some metadata that wasn't accurate. Fortunately, the combination of human review and subsequent agent reviews caught these. This is the distinction that mattered throughout the whole cataloging effort: AI-assisted research isn't the same thing as AI-verified research. The model was good at breadth and discovery; verification still required deterministic tooling and a human actually looking. It was also at this point in the process that I decided to introduce a submission desk: when I approve a visitor-submitted organization, a worker automatically opens a GitHub pull request adding it to the dataset, so I review a diff instead of a raw report. Because the data is baked into the binary, it goes live on the next deploy.

Advocacy is human. Cities are human, and only exist as the incredible, culturally diverse places they are because of the people who live in them. This work is focused on making our cities better places to live. I never forgot that, no matter how many interactions I had with Claude.

## Closing thoughts

Cities aren't static — they're made and remade every day by the people living in them, in ways no dataset fully captures. No AI, predictive text generator, or any one individual is going to be able to fully understand the specific nuances of a city. One learns and understands a city by talking to its people, listening to their specific stories. A city is its people.

Use AI, and use it aggressively where it makes you faster. But don't let it stand in for your own eyes, ears, and the community of humans who call a city their home.
