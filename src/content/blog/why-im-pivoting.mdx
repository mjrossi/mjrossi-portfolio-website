The next eighteen months are going to look like a sharper turn from the
outside than they feel from the inside.

In May I'm wrapping up five years at Heroku, and thirteen years total in
software engineering. In autumn I'm starting a two-year master's in
sustainable urban mobility — first in Lisbon, then in Barcelona. That's the
short version. The longer one is that this isn't really a pivot so much as a
slow course change finally hitting the angle it's been pointing toward for a
while.

## How this started

I grew up outside Detroit. The shape of that place — built almost entirely
around the car, with very little to fall back on if you didn't have one — is
something I've never quite stopped thinking about. It's the kind of formative
environment you don't recognize as a thesis statement at the time. You just
notice, again and again, that an absence of options is itself a kind of
option, chosen on your behalf.

I got on a plane for the first time at seventeen. The cities I started
seeing afterward — places where walking, transit, and dense urban living
were the default rather than the exception — quietly rearranged what I
thought a city was for. More travel through my twenties did more of the
same: Chicago, Seoul, a long list of others. The thread wasn't really about
transit or street design as such. It was that the way a city moves people
determines, in concrete and often unflattering ways, who has access to
what — work, health, family, public life. Cities aren't shaped by
transportation so much as *through* it.

In Chicago I started showing up to ChiHackNight, working with GTFS feeds
and transit APIs — a small personal project on top of CTA's real-time bus
tracking ended up teaching me as much about institutional decisions as it
did about data formats. In New York I got involved with Riders Alliance
and Transportation Alternatives. Reading shifted toward land use, housing,
climate adaptation, and transit-oriented development. By the time I
noticed, mobility had stopped being a thing I read about on the side; it
was the lens I was using on most of what I paid attention to.

## Why a pivot, not a side project

Software engineering has been good to me. Thirteen years across payments,
financial services, and cloud infrastructure is a long stretch of
large-scale, regulated, multi-stakeholder work, and it teaches you a lot
about reliability, trade-offs, and how decisions actually get made (and
unmade) inside big organizations. I think those skills travel — most of
mobility, in the end, is multi-stakeholder, long-horizon, institutionally
entangled work. I'm not walking away from any of that.

But there's a difference between a thing you find interesting on weekends
and a thing you'd reorganize a decade around. For a long time mobility was
the first and the engineering work was the second. The honest answer is
that the ratio has been quietly inverting for years, and at some point it
became uncomfortable to leave them in their current arrangement.

The other thing the engineering years have taught me is the limits of
purely technical solutions. The hard problems in mobility — equitable
access, climate adaptation, the relationship between land use, housing,
and transit — don't get solved by better tooling. They get solved, when
they get solved, through policy, governance, and planning frameworks I
haven't been formally trained in. That's the gap I want to close, and
it's the gap that makes this a degree program and not a side project.

## Why now

Three things lined up.

First, the program. The EIT Urban Mobility Master School's SUMT track is a
European consortium master's focused on policy, governance, and systems
thinking in sustainable urban mobility — two master's degrees and an EIT
certificate at the end of it — and it turned out to be the most precise
match I've found for what I actually want to learn. Not only transport
planning, not only urban data, but the connective tissue between policy,
infrastructure, and the institutions that decide which interventions are
even on the table. The structure matters too: studying and living in two
different cities, working on challenge-driven projects with cities and
industry partners, is much closer to how mobility transitions actually
happen than any single-university program I looked at.

Second, the timing at work. I've been at Heroku long enough to feel like
the work I cared about is in good hands, and the program starts at a point
where I can give it a real run rather than half-stepping.

Third — and this one is the more personal piece — Hilary and I have wanted
to live in Europe for a long time. The program is the right vehicle for
the career change; it also, not incidentally, makes good on a plan we've
been holding for years. As a dual Italian–US citizen, I've always had one
foot pointed across the Atlantic; this is the first arrangement of life
that's let me put both down. We'll be in Lisbon by the end of summer.

So that's the "why," at the high level. This blog is where I'll dig into
the smaller pieces — what I'm reading, what I'm noticing in the cities
I'm in, what I get wrong, and how the engineering instincts hold up
against a field that's much more about people and politics than I'm used
to.

If any of that's interesting to you, I'm glad you're here.
