The next eighteen months are going to look like a sharper turn from the outside than they feel from the inside — and honestly, anyone who's talked to me for more than 45 seconds already knew this was coming.

In May I'm wrapping up five years at Heroku, and thirteen years total in software engineering. In September I'm starting a two-year master's in sustainable urban mobility; first in Lisbon, then in Barcelona.

## How this started

I grew up outside Detroit, a place built almost entirely around the car, with very little to fall back on if you didn't have one. We didn't travel much growing up, so it was an easy assumption as a child that this is just what places were: houses separated by large roads and highways. Still, I was fascinated by it — at six years old I had an extremely strong sense of direction and spent a lot of time studying maps, looking out the window at passing scenery, and figuring out what roads and highways connected to which places and people.

At seventeen, I got on a plane for the first time. The cities I started seeing afterward were so vastly different from where I grew up — places where walking, transit, and dense urban living were the default rather than the exception. More travel through my twenties did more of the same: Chicago, London, Seoul, a long list of others.

I didn't have the vocabulary at the time to really understand what felt so different, but it did. What I realized with time was that the way a city moves people determines, in concrete and often unflattering ways, who has access to what — work, health, family, public life.

Cars are a marvel of engineering, but I also remember the sheer panic of my father — and a teenage version of me — dealing with "the car didn't start" situation. Without a friend or family member to give you a ride, you were stuck. You weren't going to work. You weren't going to the grocery store. You weren't going to that appointment. An absence of options is itself a kind of option, chosen on your behalf.

## Life in my first walkable city

At twenty-three I moved to Chicago. I sold my car. Contrary to my upbringing where the car was freedom, what I realized over years was that this felt more free. My life wasn't restricted; it opened up. I was more connected to my city, my neighborhood.

I biked as many square miles of that city as I could. I took every 'L' line, getting off in random neighborhoods to just walk and explore. The buses unlocked corners of the city. Even the Chicago suburbs, as car-dependent as they are, had Metra and cute main streets near those trains. It's hard to put into words how much I enjoyed that early exploration, and how it has continued to this day.

## Early advocacy and projects

I was working as a software engineer and eventually learned of ChiHackNight — an incredible space to meet people who cared about making their city better, and to work on a small personal project on top of CTA's real-time bus tracking. It ended up teaching me as much about institutional decisions as it did about data formats.

After moving to New York in 2019, I got involved with Riders Alliance and Transportation Alternatives. My reading shifted toward land use, housing, climate adaptation, and transit-oriented development. By the time I noticed, mobility had stopped being a thing I read about on the side; it was the lens I was using on most of what I paid attention to.

## Why a pivot, not a side project

Software engineering has been good to me, and the skills it builds travel. Much of the work in mobility is, in the end, multi-stakeholder, long-horizon, institutionally entangled work. I'm not walking away from any of that.

But there's a difference between a thing you find interesting on weekends and a thing you'd reorganize a decade around. The honest answer is that the ratio has been quietly inverting for years, and at some point it became uncomfortable to leave things in their current arrangement.

The hard problems in mobility, such as equitable access, climate adaptation, the relationship between land use, housing, and transit, don't _only_ get solved by better tooling and technology. They get solved through all of that _and_ policy, governance, and planning frameworks I haven't been formally trained in. That's the gap I want to close, and it's the gap that makes this a degree program and not a side project.

## Why now

The EIT Urban Mobility Master School's SUMT track turned out to be the most precise match I've found for what I actually want to learn — not only transport planning and urban data, but the connective tissue between policy, infrastructure, and the institutions that decide which interventions are even on the table. Studying and living in two different cities, working on challenge-driven projects with cities and industry partners, is much closer to how mobility transitions actually happen than any single-university program I looked at.

There's also a more personal piece. My wife literally put urban density commitments in her wedding vows: promising that we'll always live near bike lanes, take the Metro over a rideshare (most of the time), and a sworn moratorium on suburban sprawl. She coined "MattTA," which tells you something about how often I bring this up. We've wanted to live in Europe for a long time. As a dual Italian-US citizen I've always had one foot pointed across the Atlantic; this is the first arrangement of life that's let me put both down. We'll be in Lisbon by the end of summer.

## What's next

This blog is where I'll dig into the smaller pieces — what I'm reading, what I'm noticing in the cities I'm in, what I get wrong, and how the engineering instincts hold up against a field that's much more about people and politics than I'm used to.

If any of that's interesting to you, I'm glad you're here.
